Nagoya Moved Its Graves to Build Its Avenues

The 100-metre-wide park you crossed under the TV Tower exists because, after 1945, the city took the graveyards of 279 temples and moved them to a single hill in the east. The temples stayed where they were. Their dead went to Heiwa Park, and they are still laid out there temple by temple.

平和公園 Heiwa KōenChikusa Ward · Naka WardFacts checked November 2026

If you have been to Nagoya, you have probably walked Hisaya-ōdōri Park (久屋大通公園): the lawns, the shops of the 2020 redevelopment, the old TV Tower in the middle. It is a park strip in the centre of a road that is a hundred metres wide from kerb to kerb, which is unusual even by Japanese standards. Very few visitors ask how a city found that much empty land in its centre.

The answer is on a hill in Chikusa Ward, about half an hour away by subway and bus, in a cemetery that the English-language guides describe as a nice place for cherry blossoms. This piece connects the two ends: the avenue in town, the graves on the hill, and the temples left behind in between. You give up a sightseeing day for a walk through a working cemetery. What you get is the clearest explanation in Japan of how a burned city was rebuilt.

A quarter of the city burned名古屋空襲 · Nagoya kūshū

Nagoya was an aircraft-building city, and it was bombed accordingly. By the city's own history, 38 air raids between December 1944 and July 1945 burned about 3,850 hectares, close to a quarter of the city, destroyed more than 113,000 homes, and killed 7,802 people. The castle keep went with them. The city's peace pages count the raids differently and give a death toll of nearly 8,000.

In October 1945 the city hired a civil engineer, Tabuchi Jurō (田淵寿郎), as chief engineer for reconstruction. His plan, in the city history's words, was bold beyond what anyone imagined at the time: two avenues a hundred metres wide, one north–south and one east–west, which would split the city into quarters as firebreaks and evacuation routes, and the removal of the city's graveyards to the suburbs.

The temples stay, the graves go墓地の集団移転 · bochi no shūdan iten

Central Nagoya, like every old Japanese castle town, had temples packed into its streets, and each temple had a graveyard behind it. Land readjustment, the method Japan used to rebuild burned cities, works by taking a slice of every plot to make roads and parks. Graveyards were the one kind of land nobody could easily slice.

So the city did something no other Japanese city did on this scale. The temples would stay in town. Only their graveyards would move, all of them, to one site in the eastern hills. In June 1946 a committee of sixteen representatives of the Buddhist sects was set up to negotiate with the temples and the families. In 1947 the cemetery park was fixed in the city plan. The relocation was largely done by November 1957, and the last grave markers were moved in September 1981.

The city's figures: the graveyards of 279 temples, about 18 hectares, moved to about 147 hectares of hill. The city gives the count as 189,030; one other Japanese source writes about 19,000, and I could not settle which unit each is counting.

The avenue the graves paid for百メートル道路 · hyaku-mētoru dōro

Japan's post-war reconstruction plans called for 100-metre roads in several cities. Then the 1949 austerity budget cut the national subsidies, and only three were built: two in Nagoya and Hiroshima's Peace Boulevard. Nagoya's mayor told the city council in 1946 that they were for health, disaster prevention and the beauty of the city. Tabuchi, in his autobiography, compared them to the Champs-Élysées.

Hisaya-ōdōri runs about 1.8 km north–south through Sakae, with a park about 70 metres wide down the middle; the TV Tower went up in it in 1954. Wakamiya-ōdōri runs about 4 km east–west and could only be finished in 1964, after a prison in its path moved out of the city. Today an expressway runs above its central park.

What was left behind in town総見寺・建中寺 · Sōkenji, Kenchūji

Start in town, because the absence is the point. Sōkenji (総見寺) in Ōsu is the family temple of Oda Nobunaga's line; its graveyard went to Heiwa Park in the reconstruction. Kenchūji (建中寺) in Higashi Ward is the family temple of the Owari Tokugawa lords; the mausolea of the lords were moved too, all except that of the second lord, Mitsutomo. In both, the temple buildings stand with much less ground around them than a temple of that rank should have.

Sōkenji: 中区大須3-23-38. Kamimaezu Station (上前津駅), about 8 min walk.
Kenchūji: 東区筒井1-7-57. Kurumamichi Station (車道駅, Sakura-dōri Line), about 10 min walk.
Reality check: these are working temples. Grounds are usually open in the daytime; buildings may not be.

Heiwa Park: the cemetery laid out by temple平和公園 · Heiwa Kōen

The graves were not mixed up. Each temple was given its own block, grouped by sect, and signs with the temple's name stand throughout the grounds. Walking here is like walking a map of central Nagoya folded into a hillside: the block for a temple in Ōsu, then one from Higashi Ward, each with its old grave markers re-set in rows. One Japanese account says temples were offered about 1.3 times the area they gave up.

The city has marked a "cultural figures' graves" walk of about 4.5 km. The names on its map include Tokugawa Muneharu, the Owari lord who defied the shogun's austerity and made Nagoya fun; Hirate Masahide, Nobunaga's tutor; Matsudaira Senchiyo, a son of Tokugawa Ieyasu who died as a child; the botanist Itō Keisuke; and Chin Genpin (陳元贇), a Ming Chinese émigré of the 17th century. Tabuchi Jurō, who planned all of this, is buried here too.

To find one temple's block, ask at the rest house: 〇〇寺の墓地はどこですか (… -ji no bochi wa doko desu ka).

Heiwa-dō and the Nanjing Kannon平和堂 · Heiwa-dō

The hall at the centre of the park, Heiwa-dō, was finished in 1964 as the memorial to the cemetery relocation, and to pray for the war dead. Inside is a thousand-armed Kannon about 3.5 metres tall with a complicated history. In March 1941 Nagoya sent a large Kannon to Pilu Temple (毘盧寺) in Nanjing; in May, Pilu Temple sent this one back in return, on condition that it be enshrined in Nagoya. The statue Nagoya sent was destroyed during the Cultural Revolution. The one Nanjing sent was kept in temporary homes through the war and after, until this hall was built for it.

When: the interior opens only on a few days a year, 10:00–15:00. Remaining dates in the 2026 fiscal year: 20–22 March 2027. Outside those days you see the building only.

The honest part

This is a cemetery in use. Families come to clean graves and leave flowers, especially at the spring and autumn equinoxes and in mid-August. Walk the paths, not between the stones, and do not photograph people at a grave.

The scale works against you. The temple blocks are not signposted in English, the park is hilly, and the bus is the only easy way in. If you want a specific grave, you will need the Japanese name and some patience.

Heiwa-dō is almost always shut. Unless you come on one of its few opening days, you will see a closed hall.

The numbers do not all agree. The city's figure for graves moved, the date of "completion" and the count of air raids each differ between sources, and I have given the ones from the city's own history where I could. There is also an Aqua Tower water tower in the park with an observation room; sources disagree on when it opens, so I have left it out.

On 23 November each year a family jogging and walking event fills part of the park with about 3,000 people. Pick another day.

If you only have one day

Start in town, end on the hill. Not on a Monday, when the rest house is shut.
