Kyoto's Missing Corners

The north-east corner of the Imperial Palace wall is not there. It was folded inward so that, on paper, the palace has no corner facing the demon gate. Once you have seen that one, you start finding the same trick on shrines, houses and a department-store basement.

鬼門除け kimon-yokeKamigyō · Sakyō · KawaramachiFacts checked November 2026

You have walked the length of Kyoto Gyoen (京都御苑) on the way to somewhere else, probably from Marutamachi to Imadegawa, past the gravel and the big gates. You may even have toured the palace. What the tour does not stop at is the outside of one corner of its wall, where a carved monkey sits behind a wire net under the eaves.

This piece is about that corner and the idea behind it: that the north-east, the kimon (鬼門, "demon gate"), is where trouble comes in, and that the safest thing to do is to leave nothing there for it to hit. You give up the big-ticket sights for a day of small, odd architectural details, most of them free and none of them labelled in English. In return, the rest of Kyoto starts to look slightly different.

The idea: take the corner away鬼門 · kimon / 裏鬼門 · ura-kimon

In the Japanese version of directional lore, the north-east (ushitora, 丑寅) is the demon gate and the south-west is the "back" demon gate, ura-kimon (裏鬼門). Kyoto has worried about both since it was laid out as Heian-kyō in 794. The usual summary is that Enryaku-ji on Mount Hiei watched the north-east and Iwashimizu Hachimangū on Otokoyama watched the south-west, a pair of guards at opposite ends of a diagonal.

The detail that matters on the ground is less dramatic. The Kyoto writer Irie Atsuhiko points out that kimon-yoke (鬼門除け), the word you will see on signs, does not mean fighting demons off. It means arranging things so the north-east corner does not exist: cut it, recess it, fill it with something that is not quite building. A corner that is not there cannot be entered. Irie is sceptical of the newer word kimon-fūji (鬼門封じ, "sealing"), which implies a battle; the older approach is to step aside.

Sarugatsuji, the palace corner that folds in猿ヶ辻 · Saru-ga-tsuji

Walk the outside of the Imperial Palace's earthen wall (築地塀, tsuiji-bei) to its north-east end and the corner is notched: the wall steps inward instead of meeting at a point. The Ministry of the Environment's sign puts it plainly: the corner is the demon gate, so the wall's corner was removed and a monkey was placed there.

The monkey is under the eaves, a small wooden figure in a tall court hat holding a gohei wand. It is described as the messenger of Hiyoshi (Hie) Sannō, the mountain gods at the foot of Mount Hiei. The wire mesh in front of it has its own story: the monkey kept slipping out at night to play tricks on passers-by, so it was caged. The monkey link is a pun as much as a theology. The Hiyoshi monkey is called masaru (神猿), which can be heard as 魔去る, "evil leaves."

The corner also has a darker footnote. In 1863 the court noble Anekōji Kintomo, a leading voice for expelling foreigners, was attacked and killed near this spot. Japanese histories call it the Sarugatsuji incident.

Getting there: Imadegawa Station (今出川駅, Karasuma subway line) is the closest station for the north side of the park. Enter Kyoto Gyoen from Imadegawa-dori and walk east along the palace's north wall to its far corner.
When: The park is open 24 hours a day and free. The corner is on the outside of the palace wall, so you do not need a palace ticket or tour.
Reality check: The monkey is small, high up and behind mesh. Bring a phone zoom. Kyoto Gyoen enquiries: 075-211-6364.

Sainokami no Yashiro, the shrine on the line幸神社 · Sai-no-kami no yashiro

A few streets north-east of the palace corner, at the top of Nashinoki-dori, is a neighbourhood shrine whose name trips up even Japanese visitors. It is not "Saiwai Jinja." It is read Sai-no-kami no yashiro, after the sae no kami, the road-boundary gods who stand at village edges and crossroads.

Kamigyō Ward's own description is cautious: it was probably a road-boundary god of the old Izumoji district in the early Heian period, and its deity is Sarutahiko, now prayed to for road safety. The more popular story, repeated in Japanese guides, is that it was founded in 794 to guard the new capital's north-east. Either way, it sits on the kimon line and behaves like it: there is a carved monkey with a gohei on its shoulder by the main hall, and a sacred stone in the north-east of the grounds, fenced off. Irie also notes the cones of raked sand (立砂, tatezuna) that you may recognise from Kamigamo.

This is a small, residential shrine. Nobody will be selling anything in English, and there may be nobody there at all.

If someone is at the office and you cannot find the carving, ask: おさるさんは どこですか (o-saru-san wa doko desu ka).
Address: 303 Kōjin-chō, Teramachi-dōri Imadegawa agaru nishi-iru, Kamigyō-ku (京都市上京区寺町通今出川上る西入幸神町303).
Getting there: Demachiyanagi Station (出町柳駅, Keihan and Eizan lines), across the Kamo River and through Demachi Masugata shopping street; Japanese sources give between 8 and 15 minutes on foot.
When: No opening hours are published. Go in daylight.
Reality check: Sources disagree on exactly where the monkey sits (east side or north-east corner of the main hall). It is a working local shrine; keep voices down and do not climb into the fenced stone area.

Sekizan Zen-in, the monkey on the roof赤山禅院 · Sekizan Zen-in

Follow the line further north-east to the foot of Mount Hiei and you reach a Tendai temple that describes itself as standing to the north-east of the palace, as its "front" demon gate. On the roof of its worship hall sits another monkey, also caged, holding a gohei and a kagura bell. The temple's own page says the monkey guards the palace, the same job as the one at Sarugatsuji, from further out.

Sekizan Zen-in is in English guides, mainly for its autumn leaves. Read it here as the far end of a chain that starts at a notch in a wall.

Getting there: Shugakuin Station (修学院駅, Eizan line from Demachiyanagi), then about 20 minutes on foot east, uphill. City buses 5, 31 and 65 to Shugakuin Rikyū-michi stop are also about 20 minutes' walk.
When: Gates 6:00–17:00; the temple gives worship hours as 9:00–16:30.
Reality check: The leaf season (mid to late November) is crowded. Outside it, the walk up is quiet and the roof monkey is the main thing you came for.

The corners in ordinary streets町なかの鬼門除け · machinaka no kimon-yoke

This is the part you can do anywhere in central Kyoto, and the part almost nobody notices. Look at the north-east corner of a house, a shop, or a small office building. A surprising number have something there that is not wall:

What you seeWhy it is there
A small square or triangle framed off at the corner, filled with white gravelFills the corner with "not building." Keihan's local-history column lists it as one of the standard forms.
A nandina shrub (南天, nanten), red berries in winterIts name sounds like 難を転じる, "turning difficulty around."
Holly-leaved osmanthus (柊, hiiragi)Spiny leaves, the same plant used against demons at Setsubun.
A small decorative roof tile placed on the cornerHomes use it in place of the big demon-face tiles (鬼瓦) on temples.
The corner itself chamfered or cut awayThe "proper" version, the same idea as the palace wall.

How common is it? Japanese Wikipedia, citing a 2015 book on Japanese customs, reports that a Kyoto non-profit survey that year found around 1,100 such kimon-yoke on buildings, shops and homes in central Kyoto alone. A Kyoto Sangyo University study by Fujino Masahiro, walking the city in person, describes forms from proper cut corners to gravel and plants, and notes that on old maps the cut corner shows up clearly only at the Imperial Palace and Bukkō-ji. In other words: the grand version is rare; the household version is everywhere.

Where: Any older residential block. The streets between the palace and Demachiyanagi, and the Teramachi and Nishijin areas, are good for this.
When: December to February is best: nandina berries are red and easy to spot.
Reality check: These are people's homes. Photograph the corner from the street, not the house, and do not step onto the gravel.

The basement triangle at Shijō-Kawaramachi四条河原町の地下 · Shijō Kawaramachi no chika

The strangest example Irie describes is under one of the busiest crossings in Kyoto. In the basement of the Edion building on the Shijō-Kawaramachi corner (the former Hankyū department store), where the passage from the Hankyū Kyoto-Kawaramachi Station concourse meets the Shijō-dōri exit, he notes an awkward recess with a single unmarked door. Behind it, he writes, is a triangular space about a metre on each side, kept empty as kimon-yoke, in a spot where every square metre is worth money.

Treat this as a reading exercise rather than a sight. You are looking at a dent in a wall and a plain door. Its value is in what it shows: a commercial building in 2020s Kyoto still giving up a corner.

Getting there: Kyoto-Kawaramachi Station (京都河原町駅, Hankyū line); the recess is described as on the link between the station concourse and the Shijō-dōri exit, under the Edion building.
Reality check: This comes from a single 2024 essay. Building works can change basements; I could not confirm it is unchanged in 2026. Do not try the door.

The honest part

None of this is spectacular. The palace monkey is a small, dark carving behind mesh. Sainokami is a shrine you could walk past in thirty seconds. The basement triangle is a door. If you want temples that look like temples, this is not your day.

The street corners are the real reward, and they need patience. You will look at fifty corners for every good one, and many buildings are oriented to the street grid, which in Kyoto is close to but not exactly north-south, so "north-east" is a judgement call. Use your phone's compass.

Facts are thin in places. The founding date of Sainokami is a tradition, not a record. The 1,100 figure comes from a survey I could only find reported second-hand. And the whole subject is folk belief: Japanese writers disagree about whether the cut corner is a defence, a disguise, or just habit.

If you only have one day

Start far out and walk back in, so the last stops are the subtle ones and you have your eye in.

  1. 9:00Eizan line from Demachiyanagi Station to Shugakuin Station. Walk 20 minutes east to Sekizan Zen-in; find the monkey on the worship-hall roof.
  2. 10:30Back to Demachiyanagi. Cross the Kamo River and go through Demachi Masugata shopping street toward Sainokami no Yashiro.
  3. 11:15Sainokami no Yashiro: the monkey by the main hall, the fenced stone, the sand cones.
  4. 11:45Walk south-west into Kyoto Gyoen to the Sarugatsuji corner. Stand outside the notch and look at how the wall steps in.
  5. 12:30Lunch around Imadegawa or Teramachi.
  6. 13:30Walk south on Teramachi-dōri toward Shijō, checking north-east corners as you go. Count gravel squares and nandina; aim for five.
  7. 15:30Kyoto-Kawaramachi Station basement: find the recess between the concourse and the Shijō-dōri exit.

Running late? Drop Sekizan Zen-in and start at Sainokami at 10:30. The street walk is the part to keep.
