Why this is not another textbook course

Three things you will not get from an app or a grammar book.

🕺

Rhythm, not memorising

I am a dancer. Verb forms, pitch accent and long vowels are rhythm problems, and rhythm is learned by moving — not by staring at a chart.

🩺

The Japanese of the body

I also work as a bodyworker. 肩がこる, 腰が痛い, だるい — the words you need at a clinic, a pharmacy or a job in care. Almost no course teaches them.

✨

Onomatopoeia, properly

ぴかぴか, どきどき, ふわっと. Japanese people use these all day and no dictionary can explain them. I can show them with my body until you feel the difference.

Articles — the Japan that is not on the map

Written for people who have already done Kyoto and Shibuya. Why things in Japan are still standing, and why most of them are not.

🌊

The Blue Came From Berlin

The blue in the Great Wave is a German chemical that got cheap in the 1820s. Where Edo's print trade is still running, and why no museum can promise you the famous one.

 🏯

Ueno Was a Battlefield

The museums stand there because the government burned the hill down in one morning in 1868. Two bullet-holed gates, and a grave with the name left off.

 🌲

The Pine in the Shrine

A tree was struck by lightning in 1849 and became part of a building. Nobody has written down whether it is still in there.

 🗿

Eighteen Sleepless Nights

Those grey roadside stones are attendance records. Tokyo's oldest is from 1623, in a suburb with no tourists, signed by farmers.

 🔥

Burned in Peacetime

Nishiarai Daishi came through the firebombing. Its main hall burned down in 1966, and what everyone photographs is concrete from 1971.

 🐟

Tsukudani Was Born Twice

The preserve on every breakfast tray is named after a Tokyo island, and the island after an Osaka village. Both are still there. Almost nobody visits the second one.

 🌶️

The Pond Under the Towers

Look down from the free City Hall observatory: that strip was a pond and a geisha quarter until 1968. Across the station, Edo's soba chili is growing again.

 🐱

The Lucky Cat's Last Kiln

The earliest record of the beckoning cat puts it in Asakusa in 1852, made of Imado clay. Fifty potter households once worked here. Taitō City now counts one craftsman.

 🔥

Akihabara's Missing Shrine

Akihabara is named after a fire shrine on an empty firebreak. It moved out two years before the station opened, and once a year it still lets you walk across fire.

 🪨

The Stones That Didn't Make the Wall

Osaka Castle's rejects: stones that fell off boats on the Yodo, stayed on a beach in Ashiya, or spent 21 years as a park sign in Kobe.

 ⚙️

What Could Not Be Melted

Earthquake, firebombing, and a wartime scrap-metal order. Three sieves, three different meshes — and a rule for reading any old thing in Tokyo.
