Why a Nagoya Arcade Throws a Paris Festival

Fifteen minutes' walk from Nagoya Station, a Shōwa-era shopping arcade fills with berets, brocante stalls and accordions for one weekend each November. It is not a theme park. It is how a street that was half shuttered got its customers back, and why it picked autumn.

円頓寺 秋のパリ祭 Endōji aki no Pari-saiEndōji arcade · ShikemichiFacts checked 2 November 2026

If you have stopped in Nagoya at all, it was probably for the castle, a plate of miso katsu and maybe the rail museum, on the way to somewhere else. Between the station and the castle, along the Horikawa canal, is the district you walked past: Shikemichi's old storehouses, which English guides now cover, and right next to them a covered shopping street, Endōji Shōtengai (円頓寺商店街), which they mostly mention in a line.

On Saturday 14 and Sunday 15 November 2026 that street holds its Autumn Paris Festival. Go for the festival if you like markets. Go for the reason behind it if you have ever walked down a Japanese shōtengai with every second shutter down and wondered whether any of them come back. This one did, and the paper trail of how is unusually clear.

A temple street that ran out of customers円頓寺商店街 · Endōji shōtengai

The street grew up in front of a temple, Chōkyūzan Endōji (長久山円頓寺), which still stands on it and gave it its name. Its merchant roots go back to the early 1600s, when the entire town of Kiyosu was moved to the new castle town of Nagoya (清須越, Kiyosu-goshi). From the Edo period until the late 1960s it did what a neighbourhood street does: it sold the district its food and everyday goods, and it did well enough to be counted as one of Nagoya's three big shopping streets. The first arcade roof went up in 1964.

Then the neighbourhood stopped needing it. People moved to the suburbs and shopped at big suburban stores, and a street built on daily errands lost its reason to exist. By around 2003 it had about half the shops it had ten years earlier; Japanese reporting describes it as more than half shuttered. That is the ordinary end of the story in most Japanese towns.

Filling the shutters one building at a timeナゴノダナバンク · Nagono Dana Bank

The person most often credited is Masato Ichihara (市原正人), a local architect who runs a design office called DERO. He joined a volunteer neighbourhood group, Nagono Shitamachi-shū (那古野下町衆), and inside it a team called Nagono Dana Bank (ナゴノダナバンク), "dana" from tana, a shop, focused on reusing the empty shops and houses.

The first idea, a register of empty buildings for anyone to rent, did not work. Owners of old shophouses did not want to deal with strangers or pay for repairs. What worked was taking the risk off them. Ichihara drew up a business plan for each property and, where needed, leased the whole building himself, looked after it, and sublet it to new tenants. One example, Endōji Apartment, holds three shops. In the roughly ten years to 2018, 28 shops opened this way and 26 were still trading, the two that closed having done so for their owners' personal reasons. Rents on the street more than doubled.

Walk the street and you can see the result: old family shops side by side with a bakery, a shoemaker and cafés in old buildings. One example the Japanese press wrote up is Nagonoya (なごのや), a guesthouse and café in the building of Nishi-Asahi, a coffee shop that had run for 80 years before closing. Another is 圓谷, a shop run by the Sekiya brewery (関谷醸造) in a former rice storehouse. Shops on a street like this come and go; check what is open on the day rather than trusting any list, this one included.

Why Paris, and why November秋のパリ祭 · aki no Pari-sai

The festival idea came up in 2011 and took about a year and a half to prepare. The reason for Paris is disarmingly simple: according to the organisers, several of the people involved just liked Paris. The reason for autumn is the calendar. France's big day, the one Japanese call "Pari-sai", is 14 July. But Endōji's July was already taken. Its Tanabata festival, running since 1956, fills five days in the last week of July and is the street's biggest event. So they made an original "autumn Paris festival" instead.

The first one, in autumn 2013, had about 20 shops invited directly and another 80 stall places filled by open application. It worked well enough to become the street's second fixture, and in 2015, when the arcade roof was rebuilt, Endōji signed a sister-street agreement with the Passage des Panoramas, one of the oldest covered passages in Paris, in the 2nd arrondissement. The Paris side's chairman praised the way shopkeepers and people who love the area run the street together. The festival paused for two years during COVID and is back.

For 2026 the theme is "Fashion". Expect stalls of French-themed goods, sweets, flowers and brocante along the arcade, live music, accordion and street performance. Many visitors come in a beret or a Breton stripe. Entry is free.

Getting there: Kokusai Center Station (国際センター駅), Sakura-dōri subway line, exit 2, 5–8 min walk north. Or Marunouchi Station (丸の内駅), exit 8, 8–10 min west. From Nagoya Station (名古屋駅) about 15 min on foot.
When: Sat 14 and Sun 15 November 2026, 11:00–18:00.
Reality check: Free. The arcade is about 220 m long and gets packed on Saturday afternoon. No rain policy is published, but the street is roofed. Organiser: Endōji Shōtengai Shinkō Kumiai, 052-551-6800 (Japanese).
At a brocante stall, before you pick up something old and fragile: 手に取ってもいいですか (te ni totte mo ii desu ka).

Next door: the street widened against fire四間道 · Shikemichi

Just south of the arcade's canal end is Shikemichi, which you may already know from the guides. What matters here is why it looks the way it does. After the great fire of 1700, the road was widened to four ken, about 7 metres, to stop fire crossing it, which is where the name comes from. The Owari domain encouraged merchants to build their storehouses in earth and plaster on stone walls along the east side, facing the canal. Those are the walls you photograph.

Look up as well as along. On some roofs in this district there are yane-gami (屋根神): tiny shrines set on the roof of a house, a custom particular to the Owari area. They are easy to miss and you should not climb anything to see them.

Getting there: A few minutes' walk south from the Gojō Bridge end of the arcade.
When: A public street; any time. Best in daylight for the walls.
Reality check: Most of the storehouses are private. Cafés and restaurants in some of them have their own hours.

The honest part

The festival is a market. If you have done a dozen flea markets and craft fairs, this is another one, with a French costume on. The stallholders come from Aichi and other parts of Japan, and much of what they sell is Japanese-made "French-style" goods. The draw is the street and what happened to it, not the merchandise.

Saturday afternoon under a 220-metre roof is crowded and slow. Strollers and wheelchairs can find it hard going at peak times. The French connection is real but it lives in an agreement and a mood: do not come expecting French vendors or a French food market.

Outside festival days, Endōji is a quiet working street. Many shops are small, family-run and close early or on fixed weekdays. That is the point of it, but do not expect a lively scene at 19:00 on a Tuesday.

If you only have one day

Saturday 14 November 2026, starting from Nagoya Station. On Sunday the same plan works and is usually a little calmer in the morning.

  1. 10:15Walk from Nagoya Station towards the Horikawa canal (about 15 minutes), or one stop on the Sakura-dōri line to Kokusai Center.
  2. 10:30Shikemichi before the crowds: the storehouse walls, and the rooftops for yane-gami.
  3. 11:00Into the arcade from the Gojō Bridge end as the festival opens. Walk the full 220 metres once before buying anything.
  4. 12:30Lunch in one of the street's cafés or from the stalls. Look in at the Endōji temple itself on the way.
  5. 14:00Second pass for the music and anything you marked. Leave before the 15:00 peak if crowds bother you.
  6. 15:00Walk on north-east towards Nagoya Castle, or back to the station.

Running late? Skip Shikemichi in the morning and see it at 16:00 instead, when the light is lower and the walls look better anyway.
