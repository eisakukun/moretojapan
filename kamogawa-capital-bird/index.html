The Capital Bird Took a Thousand Years to Reach the Capital

Stand on Shijō or Sanjō bridge in winter and the river is full of small white gulls with red bills. A classical poem says this bird was never seen in Kyoto. It was right until January 1974. Now the gulls commute in every day from Lake Biwa.

ユリカモメ yurikamome · 都鳥 miyakodoriKyoto · Lake Biwa · TokyoFacts checked November 2026

You have crossed the Kamo River (鴨川, Kamogawa) a dozen times: from Gion to Kawaramachi, under the summer platforms of Pontochō, along the stepping stones at Demachiyanagi. If your earlier trips were in spring or autumn, you saw herons, ducks and black kites. Come between late autumn and mid-April and the river changes cast. Flocks of black-headed gulls (Chroicocephalus ridibundus), white with a dark smudge behind the eye and a red bill and legs, sit on the weirs and wheel round anyone standing still on a bridge.

They are not a sight you add to an itinerary. They are the same bridges you already know, at a different time of year, with a story attached that most Kyoto residents know and almost no English guide mentions: the bird has a name meaning "bird of the capital", it is the official bird of Tokyo, and it only started visiting the actual old capital fifty-two years ago.

A poem about a bird Kyoto didn't have都鳥 · miyakodori

In the Tales of Ise (伊勢物語, Ise Monogatari), a tenth-century collection, a courtier who has left the capital reaches the Sumida River on the far eastern edge of the known world. He sees white birds with red bills and legs, which the text notes are not seen in the capital, and asks the ferryman their name. "Miyakodori," says the ferryman: capital bird. The courtier answers with one of the most quoted poems in Japanese, usually attributed to Ariwara no Narihira:

名にし負はば いざ言問はむ 都鳥 わが思ふ人は ありやなしやと
If you really bear that name, capital bird, then let me ask you: is the one I love still living, or not?

The joke at the heart of it is that the "capital bird" belonged to the provinces. The bird of the poem is generally identified with the black-headed gull, and that is the name Tokyo adopted: on 1 October 1965 the metropolitan government chose the yurikamome as its official bird. The Sumida bridge in Asakusa called Kototoi (言問橋) and the driverless Yurikamome line to Odaiba both carry the poem's echo. Kyoto, meanwhile, went on without the bird for another nine years.

Since January 1974初飛来 · hatsu hirai

Kyoto Prefecture's own page for the Kamo River Park puts the first arrival in January of Shōwa 49, which is 1974. A 2012 newsletter from the prefecture's Kyoto civil engineering office says the same: until then the gull had "no connection with the Kamo," and it has come every year since. Ornithologist Wada Takeshi counted them along 9.1 kilometres of the river, from Shijō Bridge up to Hiiragino Dam, every winter from October 1984 to May 1989, in a paper published in the bird journal Strix.

Why 1974 and not before is a question the sources here do not answer. What they agree on is the pattern since: the birds appear as autumn deepens, and after mid-April they leave.

A daily commute over the hillsねぐら · negura

The Kamo is their feeding ground, not their home. Both the prefecture's park page and the civil engineering newsletter say the gulls spend the night on Lake Biwa or Osaka Bay and come to the river by day. "In winter they commute every day from Lake Biwa to the Kamo," the 2012 newsletter puts it. Lake Biwa lies on the other side of the Higashiyama hills, so the commute crosses the ridge you can see from the bridges.

That gives the river a rhythm. The birds that sit on the weirs at midday were somewhere else at dawn and will be somewhere else again by nightfall. Exact departure times vary with the light and are not published anywhere official; stand on a bridge in the last hour before sunset and watch which way the flocks go.

From Kamchatka, with rings on足環 · ashiwa

The breeding grounds are thousands of kilometres north. A 2021 column in the Dōshisha Junior High School newsletter, by a writer who took part in ringing studies on the Kamo in the 1990s, describes the birds flying from the Kamchatka Peninsula and returning there by about May. The work was a joint study with a Russian researcher, Dr. Gerasimov: birds were netted on the river, weighed, measured, and fitted with a Ministry of the Environment metal ring and a coloured plastic one before release.

Look at the legs. A gull with a coloured ring on the Kamo is part of that long-running record, and the column asked anyone who photographs one to send the picture in. In the same piece the writer noted that, that winter, the flocks were gathering south of Marutamachi Bridge "for some reason". They shift from year to year; walk the stretch from Shijō to Demachiyanagi rather than waiting at one spot.

Watching the heads turn dark夏羽 · natsubane

In winter the head is white with a dark spot behind the eye. Before they leave, the birds moult into breeding plumage and the whole head turns a chocolate brown that reads as black from a bridge: the "black-headed" of the English name. The 2012 newsletter notes that some are already in this summer plumage as they head back towards Kamchatka around May. On a late-March or early-April trip, you can see both kinds on the same weir, which is the closest thing the river offers to a calendar.

Where on the river鴨川の橋 · Kamogawa no hashi

Any bridge from Shijō north to the Demachiyanagi delta, where the Takano and Kamo rivers join, will do. The birds rest on the low weirs (the stepped concrete drops in the riverbed) and on sandbars, and fly up when someone below throws food. Riverside paths run along both banks between the bridges.

If a local stops to look with you, you can say what you're watching: ユリカモメですね (yurikamome desu ne).
Getting there: Gion-Shijō Station (祇園四条駅, Keihan) or Kyoto-Kawaramachi Station (京都河原町駅, Hankyū) are at Shijō Bridge. Demachiyanagi Station (出町柳駅, Keihan) is at the delta.
When: roughly November to mid-April, daytime. Free, open riverbank.
Reality check: numbers and favourite spots change year to year; no official count is published for visitors. Bring binoculars or a zoom lens if you want to read leg rings.

The honest part

These are gulls. If birds do nothing for you, the story is the whole attraction, and you can get that from the bridge in five minutes. The flocks can be smaller than older photos suggest, and on a given day they may be up the river where you aren't. The sources used here give the arrival year and the commute, but no recent counts, so this article does not claim the population is rising or falling.

Don't feed them. People do, and the birds respond, but the nature photographer Imamori Mitsuhiko, writing about Lake Biwa's gulls in 2023, notes that feeding once seen as kindness is no longer thought good for the birds or the ecosystem. And the commute itself is something you infer rather than see: you will watch flocks lift and head east at dusk, not a line of birds crossing the ridge.

If you only have one day

  1. 9:30Start on Shijō Bridge (四条大橋). Look north at the weirs, then walk down to the west-bank path.
  2. 10:00Walk north on the riverside path past Sanjō to Marutamachi, checking each weir. Look at legs for coloured rings.
  3. 11:30Carry on to the Demachiyanagi delta and cross the stepping stones.
  4. 12:30Lunch around Demachiyanagi.
  5. 14:00Optional: from Sanjō-Keihan Station take the Tōzai Line through to the Keihan Keishin Line for Biwako-Hamaotsu Station (びわ湖浜大津駅) and look at the lake side of the commute from the Ōtsu harbour front. Check the through-train times before you go.
  6. 16:00Back on Sanjō or Shijō Bridge for the last hour of light, and watch which way the flocks leave.

Running late? Skip Ōtsu. Shijō Bridge at midday and again before sunset is the whole idea in two visits.
