The Hill Behind Atsuta

At the north-west corner of Atsuta Shrine there is a wooded hill in a public park that most visitors walk past on the way to the subway. It is the largest keyhole-shaped tomb in the Tōkai region, 151 metres long, built in the early sixth century and thought to belong to the family who first kept the shrine.

断夫山古墳 Danpusan Kofun · 白鳥古墳 Shiratori KofunAtsuta Ward, NagoyaFacts checked 24 November 2026

You have probably done Atsuta Jingū (熱田神宮) already: the giant camphor trees, the treasure hall, the story that the Kusanagi sword, one of the three imperial regalia, is kept here and nobody may see it. Maybe you had hitsumabushi afterwards. What the shrine visit leaves out is the question of who was here before the shrine was famous, and why the sword was in this place at all.

The answer is buried in two mounds a short walk away. Danpusan Kofun (断夫山古墳) and Shiratori Kofun (白鳥古墳) are both keyhole-shaped tombs of the early 500s, both linked to the Owari (尾張), the local ruling family, and both wrapped in a legend that ties them to the sword's story. One is the biggest of its kind in the whole Tōkai region. You cannot climb either. This is a piece about looking at a hill properly.

Why this and not another day at the shrine熱田神宮の隣 · next door to Atsuta

The shrine tells you its own story. The mounds tell you the one underneath it: an early ruling family on what was then the edge of Ise Bay, rich enough to raise a 151-metre earthwork. Danpusan is free, in a public park, a few minutes from the shrine. The trade-off is that there is almost nothing to read on site in English and nothing to enter. If you need a building or a museum case, this is not your morning.

Danpusan Kofun: the size of it断夫山古墳 · Danpusan Kofun

Danpusan is a zenpō-kōen-fun (前方後円墳), the keyhole shape: a round mound at the back, where the burial is, joined to a square front. The figures most sources agree on: 151 metres long, the round part about 80 metres across and 13 metres high, the front about 116 to 120 metres wide and 16 metres high, built in three tiers. English Wikipedia notes the original length may have been nearer 160 metres, judged from the moat. Aichi Prefecture dates it to the first half of the sixth century. It is the largest keyhole tomb in Tōkai, and it has never been excavated inside by modern methods.

Notice the odd proportion. The front is taller and wider than the round back. That is typical of late keyhole tombs, and it is why the mound, seen from the park, reads as a long wooded ridge rather than a neat circle.

Who is in it尾張氏 · the Owari

Two answers, and it is worth keeping them apart.

The archaeological one: Aichi Prefecture describes it as a tomb connected to the Owari no Muraji (尾張連), the clan who ruled this coast and who were Atsuta's early priests. One proposal names the clan head Owari no Kusaka (尾張連草香). The Nihon Shoki records a daughter of his, Menoko-hime (目子媛), as a consort of Emperor Keitai, who reigned in the early sixth century. If the attribution is right, this is the tomb of a man whose grandsons sat on the throne. Nobody can confirm it without digging, and nobody is digging.

The shrine's one: the Atsuta tradition says it is the grave of Miyazu-hime (宮簀媛), the local princess Yamato Takeru married. In the Kojiki he leaves the Kusanagi sword with her and goes off to fight the god of Mount Ibuki, and dies. The sword stays here, and that is the founding story of Atsuta Shrine. One explanation of the name Danpusan, "the mountain of the severed husband", is that she never remarried. It is one theory among several; in the Edo period the hill had a different name altogether.

The two stories are not in competition. The legend explains why the shrine guarded the hill; the archaeology explains why there was a powerful family here to build it.

The hill nobody could climb, except one day a year三月三日 · the third of March

In the Edo period the mound was part of the shrine's sacred ground and closed to the public. According to Edo-period guidebooks cited by the Japanese travel site tabi-mag, the exception was the third day of the third month, when people were allowed up to look out over Atsuta beach at low tide. That view is gone. Land reclamation has pushed the shoreline far to the south, and what you would see now is roads and apartment blocks.

The shrine managed the mound until the end of the Second World War. After the war it passed to Nagoya City, and in 1980 to Aichi Prefecture, which runs it now as part of Atsuta Jingū Park (熱田神宮公園). It became a National Historic Site in 1987, and English Wikipedia records the protected area being enlarged in October 2024. The stone-walled moat you see is a post-war restoration; the original moat is buried.

Today, visitors may not cross to the mound. Stepping stones lead across the moat, and the Japanese guides are explicit that standing on the mound is not allowed, to protect it. You walk around it.

Getting there: Jingū-nishi Station (神宮西駅, Meijō subway line), exit 4, then about 10 minutes north per Aichi Prefecture; Nishi-Takakura Station (西高蔵駅), exit 2, about 5 minutes south. Other guides give 5–7 minutes from Jingū-nishi. It sits in Atsuta Jingū Park, across the road from the shrine's north-west side.
When: A public park; walk the moat in daylight. Free.
Reality check: No entry onto the mound. Park office: 052-681-5204 (Japanese). Free parking for about 50 cars if you are driving.

Shiratori Kofun: the swan's grave白鳥古墳 · Shiratori Kofun

South of Danpusan, past Jingū-nishi Station, is the smaller of the pair. Shiratori Kofun is a 70-metre keyhole tomb, also put in the early sixth century and also thought to belong to an Owari clan head. The tradition here is bigger: Atsuta calls it the tomb of Yamato Takeru himself, the White Swan Mausoleum (白鳥御陵). In the legend he died on the road home and turned into a white bird; the bird came down here.

Until the Edo period the mound sat inside the precinct of a temple, Hōji-ji (法持寺). Its moat is gone. Gilt-bronze horse fittings found there are a designated prefectural cultural property. Like Danpusan, you look at it from outside; the mound is closed for safety and protection.

Once a year, on 8 May, Atsuta Shrine holds a tomb rite, the Goryōbo-sai (御陵墓祭), at both mounds. Japanese guides mention it but do not describe a public part, so treat it as something you might see, not an event.

Getting there: Jingū-nishi Station, about 5 minutes on foot. From Danpusan, walk south past the shrine's west side; check the route on a map (白鳥古墳, Atsuta-ku Shiratori).
When: Viewable from outside in daylight. 8 May: the shrine's tomb rite.
Reality check: Small, fenced, and surrounded by housing. Five minutes is enough.

If you want to ask a local which way:

白鳥古墳はどっちですか (Shiratori kofun wa dotchi desu ka). Which way is Shiratori Kofun?

Where the finds went埴輪 · haniwa

The clay figures and cylinders that once lined Danpusan's terraces did not stay on the hill. Haniwa from the site are held by the Nagoya City Museum, the Nagoya City Miharashidai Archaeological Museum and the Nanzan University Anthropological Museum. What is on display changes, and at least one of these has had long renovation closures, so check each museum's current status before making a trip for them. They are the only part of the tomb you can stand next to.

The honest part

A kofun you cannot climb looks like a wooded hill behind a moat. If you have seen the big tombs in Sakai or Nara, Danpusan will not compete; at ground level you will not see the keyhole shape at all. You see it on a map, or in the diagram above. That is the whole experience, and it is free, and it takes twenty minutes.

On-site explanation is limited and in Japanese. The best context is the shrine's own Kusanagi story, which you probably heard inside, and this page.

Shiratori is a small fenced mound among houses. The 8 May rite is not a spectacle. And the names on both tombs, a princess and a hero, are traditions; the people actually buried there are probably Owari clan heads whose names are guesses.

Skip this if you are short of time in Nagoya and want something to look at. Go if you have already done Atsuta and want to know what it was standing on.

If you only have one day

Really a morning, done as an add-on to Atsuta Shrine. Any season works.

  1. 9:00Meitetsu from Nagoya Station to Jingū-mae Station (神宮前駅). Atsuta Shrine is about 3 minutes' walk.
  2. 9:10The shrine, as before, but this time ask yourself where its first priests were buried.
  3. 10:10Leave by the north-west side and cross to Atsuta Jingū Park. Walk the full moat of Danpusan Kofun, slowly. Find the corner where the front of the mound, the taller part, faces you.
  4. 10:50Walk south, past Jingū-nishi Station, to Shiratori Kofun. Five minutes there.
  5. 11:20Continue south-west to Miya-no-watashi Park, the old ferry landing of the Tōkaidō. In the Edo period, this is where the sea began.
  6. 12:00Lunch in the Atsuta area. Then Denmachō Station (伝馬町駅) back to the centre.

Running late? Drop Shiratori. Danpusan is the one that matters.
