Minamiza · east end of Shijō Bridge, Kyoto · late November

The Morning Kyoto Hangs Up Its Actors

Once a year, just before December, the Minamiza theatre puts the names of every star in its winter kabuki run on the front of the building, painted on cypress boards in a script designed to look like a full house. It takes one morning. The boards stay up for a month.

まねき上げ Maneki-age南座 Minamiza · 吉例顔見世興行Facts checked October 2026

You have walked across Shijō Bridge into Gion more than once, probably at dusk, heading for Hanamikōji. The building on your right at the end of the bridge, with the gabled roof and the tower on top, is the Minamiza (南座), Kyoto's kabuki theatre. For most of the year its front is just a front. In the last week of November it changes: a row of wooden boards goes up across the facade, each carrying one actor's name in thick black brushwork. Kyoto people take it as the signal that December has started, a few days early.

Why this rather than seeing kabuki in Tokyo? You don't need a ticket, a seat or any kabuki knowledge to watch the boards go up. What you give up is certainty. The 2026 date had not been announced when this was written, and the event is over in a morning.

A theatre's New Year in December顔見世 · kaomise

The boards advertise the Kaomise (顔見世), literally "face-showing". In the Edo period, actors signed one-year contracts with a single theatre, running from November to the end of the following October. Each November, every theatre opened its season with a performance that showed off its new company. That made November the theatre world's New Year, and the Kaomise its first show.

The contracts are gone but the date survived, shifted by a month. Since Shochiku took over the Kabukiza in Tokyo in 1913, it has run a joint east–west company at the Kabukiza in November and at the Minamiza in December. Japanese sources call the Minamiza's the oldest Kaomise, and say the theatre kept it going even through the Pacific War. The 2025 run went from 1 to 25 December.

What the boards areまねき · maneki

Each maneki (まねき, "beckoning") is a single plank of hinoki cypress about 1.8 metres long and 30 to 32 centimetres wide, cut with a small roof on top. On it goes an actor's name and crest in Kanteiryū (勘亭流), the kabuki house script. The letters are fat and packed edge to edge with almost no white left, because empty space on the board would mean empty seats in the house; the wish is for a theatre full "from corner to corner". The strokes curl inward, which Kansai Walker's explainer reads as pulling the audience inside.

They are written fresh each year, and not at the theatre. Kyoto Shimbun's reports place the writing at Myōden-ji (妙傳寺), a temple in Sakyō Ward, where a calligrapher works through the cast in the temple's guest hall in November. Refined sake is mixed into the ink; one report says for the sheen, another for purification. In 2013 there were 64 boards.

Read across the row and you are reading a ranking. Japanese still calls a handsome leading man a nimaime (二枚目, "second board") and a comic actor a sanmaime (三枚目, "third board"). The usual explanation is that those were their positions on theatre signboards like these. It is a well-worn story rather than a proven one, but it is the one Kyoto tells.

Watching them go upまねき上げ · maneki-age

The raising, maneki-age (まねき上げ), happens in the last week of November. Reported dates cluster on the 25th and 26th: Kyoto Shimbun put it on the morning of 25 November in 2013, and a wire report dated it to 26 November in 2025. The boards are lifted onto the front of the theatre and fixed in a line. There is no admission and no ceremony to sit through. You stand across the street and watch the names appear.

Stand on the north side of Shijō-dōri, or at the Gion end of Shijō Bridge, where you can see the whole facade at once. Look up as well as along: the yagura (櫓), the drum-tower box on the roof, is the old sign of a theatre licensed by the shogunate. By 1893 the Minamiza was the last of the licensed theatres still flying one, and it is renewed every year along with the big lantern over the entrance, which a Higashiyama lantern maker, Kojima Shōten, makes by hand.

To ask a neighbour whether it is today, point at the theatre: まねき上げは今日ですか (maneki-age wa kyō desu ka).
Getting there: Gion-Shijō Station (祇園四条駅), Keihan Line, exit 6: the theatre is directly outside. Kyoto-Kawaramachi Station (京都河原町駅), Hankyu Line, exit 1, about 3 min across the bridge. Address: 東山区四条大橋東詰.
When: Usually 25 or 26 November, in the morning. The 2026 date had not been published when this was checked; watch Shochiku's Minamiza pages or Kyoto news in mid-November.
Reality check: It is a work crew on a narrow, busy pavement, with press cameras. Arrive early, keep out of the way of the theatre staff, and do not stand in the bus stop.

If you miss the morning顔見世の師走 · kaomise no shiwasu

The boards stay up for the run, so the whole of December gives you the facade in its winter state. The run itself needs a ticket. In 2024 it ran from 1 to 22 December, with the first programme at 10:30 and the second at 16:00; the 2026 schedule and cast had not been published when this was checked.

On particular days in December, the geiko and maiko of Kyoto's five geisha districts come to the Kaomise together, filling the side boxes in formal kimono. This is the kagai sōken (花街総見), the districts' group attendance. By custom they wear hairpins shaped like small maneki and sometimes go backstage to have their favourite actor sign one. The dates change each year, so treat it as luck if you see it rather than something to plan around.

Getting there: Same as above. The building itself dates from 1929 and is a Registered Tangible Cultural Property; it reopened in 2018 after seismic work.
When: Boards up from the raising until the end of the Kaomise run. Performances in December; dates and times for 2026 to be announced.
Reality check: Tickets are sold in advance through Shochiku; check early. Without a ticket, the facade is the experience. The theatre holds 1,082 seats, smaller than the Kabukiza's 1,964.

The honest part

The raising is short and plain. It is a crew with ladders and a frame, not a ritual with music, and if you arrive late you will see a finished row and nothing happening. Because the date is only confirmed close to the day, a trip planned around it can miss it by twenty-four hours.

Kanteiryū is hard to read even for Japanese readers who don't follow kabuki. You will not read the names off the boards; look up the cast list on your phone and match the crests instead.

Gion in late November is also peak autumn-leaf season. Shijō-dōri is crowded all day, and the pavement in front of the theatre is narrow. If crowds are the thing you came to Kyoto to avoid, come in the second week of December instead, when the boards are still up and the leaf crowds have gone.

If you only have one day

Assume the raising falls on the day you pick, and confirm it the week before.

  1. 7:45Keihan to Gion-Shijō Station, exit 6. Check the theatre front: if workers and boards are out, today is the day.
  2. 8:00Cross to the north pavement and watch the boards go up. Stay as long as it holds you.
  3. 10:00Walk onto Shijō Bridge and look back east at the finished row with the yagura above it.
  4. 12:00Lunch somewhere off Shijō-dōri; the main street is at its most crowded now.
  5. 14:00Find this year's cast list on Shochiku's site and match names to crests from your photos.
  6. 16:30Back to the bridge as it gets dark, when the big lantern is lit under the boards.

Running late? Skip the morning entirely and do only the 16:30 view; the boards will be there.
