The Green Named After a Village

You have eaten komatsuna in a convenience-store salad, a ramen bowl and a hotel breakfast without thinking about the name. It is a place name. The place is a residential corner of eastern Tokyo that still grows about four in every ten bunches harvested in the city.

小松菜 komatsunaShin-Koiwa · Komatsugawa · Edogawa CityFacts checked 24 November 2026

On your third trip to Tokyo the city stops being a list of neighbourhoods and starts being a list of things you eat every day. Komatsuna (小松菜) is one of them: the dark, flat-stemmed green in the oshitashi side dish, in the miso soup, in the stir-fry at the counter lunch. Shops label it plainly, 小松菜, and the name is not a description of the leaf. It means "the green from Komatsu", and Komatsu is Komatsugawa (小松川), a pair of old villages on the east side of the city, now part of Edogawa City (江戸川区).

This piece is about going to where the name comes from: a small neighbourhood shrine twelve minutes from Tokyo Station that tells the naming story, and a ward that still farms the green between apartment blocks. Nothing here is grand. What you get is the answer to a question you never asked about something you have eaten a hundred times, and a winter reason to go east of the Sumida instead of west.

Why this and not Tsukiji築地の代わりに · instead of the market

If your interest in Tokyo is food, you have already done the outer market, a depachika basement, maybe Kappabashi. All of those sell food that arrives from somewhere else. Edogawa is one of the few places inside the 23 wards where a vegetable you know is still a working crop, and where the name on the label points at the ground you are standing on. The trade-off: it is a quiet residential ward, the shrine is small, and you will see more apartment buildings than fields.

The story the ward tells「小松菜」命名のお話 · komatsuna meimei no ohanashi

Edogawa City's own version is short and is introduced as a folk tale, not as history. A shogun came out to the Komatsugawa area to hunt with hawks. At lunchtime there was nothing to cook, so the locals put a winter green picked around East and West Komatsugawa villages into a clear soup (すまし汁, sumashi-jiru) and served it. The shogun liked it and named it after the place.

Which shogun? The ward says the eighth, Tokugawa Yoshimune, is the usual answer, and then adds that some say it was the fifth, Tsunayoshi. Later retellings, including a local food-education text and the English Wikipedia entry, put the visit in 1719 and place the soup at a shrine. Visitors' reviews add a rice cake to the soup and give the priest a name. None of those details appear on the ward's page, and the shrine has no official website that I could find to confirm them. Treat the date and the menu as the story's later decorations.

What is documented is older than any of this embroidery and less romantic. The Shinpen Musashi Fudoki-kō (新編武蔵風土記稿), the shogunate's gazetteer of the province finished in 1830, notes that the best greens came from around Komatsugawa and that people called them komatsuna. The ward quotes that line itself. So by the late Edo period the name was already a place name worn by a vegetable, whoever first said it.

The shrine at the end of the story新小岩 間々井香取神社 · Shin-Koiwa Mamai Katori Jinja

The place the ward points to is Shin-Koiwa Mamai Katori Shrine (新小岩間々井香取神社), in Chūō 4-chōme, south of Shin-Koiwa Station (新小岩駅). Edogawa's November 2026 press release calls it "the place known as where Lord Yoshimune named komatsuna". Signs and listings also call it Shin-Koiwa Katori Shrine or by its protective title, 厄除 香取神社. Do not confuse it with Katori Jingū in Chiba, the head shrine of the name, which is an hour and a half further east and has nothing to do with vegetables.

It is a neighbourhood shrine, and that is the point. The ward's booklet describes it enshrining komatsuna as a guardian deity of the crop (小松菜産土神), and visitors report a stone marking the komatsuna connection and a board telling the story inside the grounds. Next door, according to the same visitors, is the site of the priest's family house, marked by a stone at its gate. It is a private home. Some reviews say viewing has been possible on certain days or by arrangement; I could not confirm any current arrangement, so look from the street.

English Wikipedia says the shrine offers komatsuna on New Year's Eve and hands it to worshippers on New Year's Day. That is plausible for a shrine with this story and it would make the first days of January the best time to go, but I found no Japanese source confirming it. Ask at the shrine office if it is open:

小松菜の碑はどこですか (komatsuna no hi wa doko desu ka). Where is the komatsuna stone?
Getting there: Tokyo Station → JR Sōbu Rapid Line (総武快速線) to Shin-Koiwa Station, 12 min, ¥209 with an IC card. Then south on foot; visitors put it at about 15 minutes. Search 間々井香取神社 or 新小岩 香取神社 on the map, not just "Katori Shrine".
When: The grounds are open in daytime. Shrine office hours are not published online that I could find.
Reality check: No English signage reported. A small shrine in a residential street: twenty minutes is plenty.

A crop, not a souvenir江戸川区の小松菜 · Edogawa no komatsuna

Most famous Edo vegetables survive as heritage seed and a plaque. Komatsuna did the opposite: it became ordinary. Edogawa still grows it at scale, in greenhouses and small plots between housing, several crops a year. The ward's figures, from Tokyo's 2023 crop survey, are 2,631 tonnes harvested in Edogawa out of 6,465 tonnes for the whole of Tokyo, about 40 percent, and the ward says it has been first in Tokyo for many years. In 2025 it counted 262 farming households growing it.

The ward's own crop notes say the season that matters is winter: it is eaten year-round, but it tastes sweeter after frost. They also call it indispensable in Tokyo-style zōni (雑煮), the New Year soup with rice cake, which is why demand peaks at the end of December. That is the honest link between the shogun story and the present: a winter green in a clear soup with mochi is still what Tokyo eats on 1 January.

Eating it where it is grown小松菜グルメスタンプラリー · komatsuna gurume sutanpu rarī

The ward runs a winter campaign that, for once, suits a visitor. The Komatsuna Gourmet Stamp Rally started on 1 November 2026 and runs to 31 January 2027, across 51 places in the ward: 47 shops and restaurants serving dishes made with local komatsuna, and 4 farm stands. You collect sticker stamps, and depending on how many you collect you can enter a prize draw. Prizes listed include a pair stay at Hotel Seaside Edogawa and a komatsuna harvesting session.

The guide is a free A5 booklet called Komatsuna-ryoku (小松菜力。, "Komatsuna Power"), now in its thirteenth edition, listing about ninety komatsuna dishes. It is handed out at Edogawa City Hall, the ward's branch offices and the listed shops, and can be downloaded from the ward's website. It is in Japanese only. You do not need to win anything: the booklet is simply the most reliable list of where to eat komatsuna in the ward this winter, from bakeries to ramen.

At a listed shop, if you want the sticker:

スタンプラリーのシールをください (sutanpu rarī no shīru o kudasai). Please give me the stamp-rally sticker.
Where: 51 places across Edogawa; the booklet has the map. Pick up a copy at the first listed shop you reach.
When: 1 November 2026 – 31 January 2027. Individual shops keep their own hours and holidays, and many close over New Year.
Reality check: The prize draw is aimed at residents; how prizes are delivered to someone without a Japanese address is not stated. The booklet and the lunch are the useful part.

Buying a bunch from the field農産物直売所 · nōsanbutsu chokubaijo

Edogawa publishes a map of farm stands, grouped in five districts. The central district around the shrine lists about a dozen, one of them named Komatsuna-kun (こまつなくん). The map does not say which stands sell komatsuna on a given day, what hours they keep, or whether anyone is there, so take cash in coins and expect some to be shut or empty.

The one stand with published hours is the JA cooperative's Edochan Shop (えどちゃんショップ), which sells ward-grown vegetables and flowers. It is moving: at its old Kita-Kasai site it opens Tuesdays and Fridays, 10:00–13:00, and from 28 November 2026 it is scheduled to reopen in a new building, Smile Plaza Kamata (すまいるプラザ鎌田), at Edogawa 1-11-16, opening Tuesday, Wednesday, Friday and Saturday, 10:00–14:00. Both sets of hours end when it sells out, and the new ones are still marked as planned.

小松菜ありますか (komatsuna arimasu ka). Do you have komatsuna today?
Getting there: Smile Plaza Kamata is in another part of the ward, at Edogawa 1-chōme (江戸川1丁目). Put the address into a map app for the train-and-bus route; it is not a walk from the shrine.
When: New site from 28 Nov 2026 (planned), Tue/Wed/Fri/Sat 10:00–14:00 (planned), until sold out.
Reality check: You are in a hotel. Buy one bunch for the room, or skip this and eat it at a restaurant instead.

The honest part

This is a story about a name, and the most famous part of the story is the least certain. The ward itself calls it a folk tale and names two possible shoguns. If you need a verified event to justify a trip, there isn't one: what is verified is that the name was in use by 1830 and that the ward grows a lot of it now.

The shrine is small and the priest's house is private. You will not find a museum, an English panel or a gift shop selling komatsuna-shaped anything. The fields are scattered and mostly in greenhouses; you are more likely to see plastic tunnels behind a fence than a green valley.

The rally booklet is Japanese only, and shop hours and holidays vary. Go on a weekday in December, before the New Year closures, and do not plan around one particular restaurant.

If you do not care where a vegetable's name comes from, skip this. If you like knowing why things are called what they are, it is the cheapest, shortest food pilgrimage in Tokyo.

If you only have one day

Really half a day, on a weekday in December, from central Tokyo.

  1. 10:00Sōbu Rapid from Tokyo Station to Shin-Koiwa Station, 12 minutes. If you can, pick up the Komatsuna-ryoku booklet first by downloading it from the Edogawa City site.
  2. 10:20Walk south to Mamai Katori Shrine, about 15 minutes. Find the komatsuna stone and the board; look at the stone by the gate of the priest's house next door from the street.
  3. 11:15Walk back toward Shin-Koiwa. Check the booklet's map for the nearest listed shop and get a paper copy and your first sticker there.
  4. 12:00Lunch at a listed restaurant: a dish made with Edogawa komatsuna.
  5. 13:30On a Tuesday, Wednesday, Friday or Saturday from 28 November, head across the ward to Smile Plaza Kamata (route from a map app) for the JA shop, before its 14:00 close if it has not sold out. Otherwise go straight back.
  6. 15:00Back in central Tokyo. Order the oshitashi at dinner and say where it is from.

Running late? Drop the farm shop. The shrine and one lunch are the whole idea.
