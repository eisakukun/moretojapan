The Radish Behind Kiyosu Castle

The fat white daikon with the green shoulder, the one in every winter oden pot in Japan, is said to trace back to one farming hamlet a short walk from the castle where Oda Nobunaga rose to power. The hamlet still grows the old line, and it still marks the spot with a stone.

宮重大根 Miyashige daikonKiyosu, AichiFacts checked November 2026

You have done Nagoya Castle, eaten miso-katsu and hitsumabushi, maybe taken the train out to Inuyama. Kiyosu Castle (清洲城, Kiyosu-jō) is the next ring out: two stops from Nagoya on the JR line, a keep on the Gojō River, the castle Oda Nobunaga ruled from before he moved his base north. Most people who go look at the keep, take the photo from the bridge, and leave.

This piece is about the fields behind it. In Haruhi (春日), a district on the north-east side of Kiyosu city, a hamlet called Miyashige (宮重) gave its name to a radish. Kiyosu's own city materials say that more than half of the daikon sold in Japan today belongs to its line. You get a 20-minute castle visit plus something nobody in your hotel will have heard of in English. You give up convenience: the vegetable is seasonal, the shops that sell it are small, and the stone that marks its birthplace has no published address.

A castle town that left清須越 · Kiyosu-goshi

The reason Kiyosu is now fields and not a city is a single decision. In 1610 Tokugawa Ieyasu began building Nagoya Castle on the plateau to the south-east and moved the town of Kiyosu there wholesale: temples, shops and households. Nagoya's city government dates the move as largely finished by 1613. The old castle was taken apart for materials. The keep you see today is a mock keep built in 1989; there is no original building on the site.

What stayed behind was the soil. The Owari plain around the Gojō River is flat and low, and Aichi Prefecture describes the Miyashige area specifically as fertile ground. When the samurai and merchants left for Nagoya, the farmers did not.

Getting there: Nagoya Station (名古屋駅) → JR Tōkaidō Line to Kiyosu Station (清洲駅), or Meitetsu Nagoya Line to Shin-Kiyosu Station (新清洲駅). Both are a 15-minute walk from the castle. Local trains only; check the timetable for the stopping service.
When: Keep open 9:00–16:30. Closed Mondays (the next weekday if Monday is a holiday) and December 29–31.
Cost: ¥400 adults for the keep.

A lord, a rest stop, a bowl of radish宮重大根 · Miyashige daikon

The name comes with a story, and the sources are honest that it is a story. Aichi Prefecture's traditional-vegetable page, citing a collection of local legends from Nishikasugai district, says that in the Hōei era (1704–1711) the lord of Owari went hawking near today's Kitanagoya. On the way back he rested at the house of the Miyashige village headman, Kondō Shōemon, who served him daikon grown nearby, simmered. The lord liked it, told them to call it Miyashige daikon, and from then on the village sent radishes to the castle every year. Kiyosu city's version makes the dish furofuki daikon, thick rounds simmered soft and eaten with miso.

The written record is older than the legend's date suggests. Kiyosu's food-education bulletin of September 2023 notes that an "Owari daikon" grown in Miyashige appears in the regional gazetteer, and that the naturalist Kaibara Ekiken listed it in his Yamato Honzō (大和本草), published in 1709. What made it prized was not only taste: the bulletin says the root was thick, well-shaped and soft-fleshed, the kind of vegetable that looks right when you send it to a lord.

If you only learn one label, learn 青首 (aokubi), "blue neck". It describes the pale green shoulder that pushes up out of the soil. Miyashige is the aokubi type, and you will see the word on supermarket daikon all over Japan.

How an Owari radish got to Edo練馬大根 · Nerima daikon

The second story ties Kiyosu to Tokyo. Nerima daikon, the long radish that made a Tokyo ward famous and filled Edo's pickle barrels, is said to descend from Miyashige. Kiyosu's bulletin quotes a gazetteer of Tokyo's old Kita-Toshima district: the shogun Tokugawa Tsunayoshi, suffering from beriberi, recuperated in the village of Shimo-Nerima and, having heard of the virtues of the Owari radish, had its seed sent from Owari to be grown there. The bulletin's own reading is careful: Miyashige seed seems to have suited the Nerima soil and grew longer and larger there.

Treat the Tsunayoshi part as a legend that both towns are happy to tell. The plant logic behind it is solid and is the real point of the bulletin: a seed taken somewhere new, grown, and re-selected year after year becomes a local variety. Japan now has more than a hundred named daikon. Several of them are cousins from this one hamlet.

Why you have never seen the original純種子保存会 · seed preservation society

Here is the twist. The line that fathered half the daikon in Japan nearly disappeared from its own fields. According to Kiyosu city, from the late 1940s onward disease and changing tastes pushed the old Miyashige out; growers switched to newer varieties, many of them its own descendants. In the Heisei era a group of local growers formed the Miyashige Daikon Pure Seed Preservation Society (宮重大根純種子保存会), selected roots that were true to type, saved their seed, and brought back a radish close to the old one. Aichi made it the first entry on its list of traditional vegetables.

The city also kept a second, stranger survivor. Aichi's agricultural experiment station used to sit in the old town of Kiyosu, and around 1966 it crossed Miyashige with a disease-resistant variety called Mino Wase (美濃早生). The result, Daikon Kiyosu (だいこんきよす), has a white neck and more bite. It was never sold. In 2008 the city germinated seed the prefecture had kept in storage, and from 2009 it has been grown properly, mostly for school lunches. Kiyosu describes it as sharper than ordinary daikon, good grated or pickled. In other words, a vegetable bred for farmers in the 1960s reached the public as a children's lunch in the 2010s.

At a farm stand or the castle market, ask: 宮重大根はありますか (Miyashige daikon wa arimasu ka). If they point at a white-necked one, it is Daikon Kiyosu, which is the other half of the story.

Where to find the radish, and the stone発祥之地趾 · birthplace stone

A marker reading 宮重大根発祥之地趾 stands in Haruhi Miyashige-chō (春日宮重町): "site of the birthplace of Miyashige daikon". The photo in the city's 2023 bulletin shows a tall black pillar with an explanation board, standing at the edge of open fields. Neither the city nor the prefecture publishes an address. Paste the kanji into your map app and see whether a pin comes up before you set out; if it does not, the castle and the furofuki at home are the trip, and the stone is a bonus.

For the vegetable itself, Kiyosu city lists two places that sell Miyashige daikon: the farm shop Agri Marché Haruhi (アグリマルシェはるひ) and the Yoshizuya supermarket's Kiyosu branch (ヨシヅヤ清洲店). That list is several years old and gives no hours.

Season: Sown when the weather cools in autumn; harvest starts around November.
Market day: The Kiyosu tourism association lists a castle morning market on the first Sunday of each month (second Sunday in January) on the promenade by the castle plaza. In 2026 that is Sunday, December 6. Whether local vegetables are on the stalls is not published — check before you go.
Reality check: No address for the stone, no hours for the farm shop. Ask the Kiyosu tourism association (清須市観光協会) at the castle.

Kiyosu Furusato no Yakata清洲ふるさとのやかた

Across from the castle is the town's visitor building, Kiyosu Furusato no Yakata, with a souvenir counter and, in the basement, the Kiyosu armour workshop (清洲甲冑工房), free to look into on Tuesday, Thursday and Saturday afternoons. It is not a vegetable shop, but it is the place to ask, in Japanese, where Miyashige is sold this week and whether the stone is walkable from there.

To ask the way: 宮重大根の碑はどこですか (Miyashige daikon no hi wa doko desu ka).
Getting there: Next to Kiyosu Castle, 清須市清洲古城479-1. Same stations, 15 minutes on foot.
When: 9:00–17:00. Closed Mondays (the next day if Monday is a holiday) and December 29–31. Armour workshop Tue, Thu, Sat 13:00–16:00.

Eat it the way the lord did風呂吹き大根 · furofuki daikon

You have had daikon in oden a dozen times. Furofuki is the simpler dish the Miyashige legend names: thick rounds simmered until soft in kombu stock, then topped with sweet miso. If you have a kitchen in your rental, buy one Miyashige root, cut three-centimetre rounds, and simmer them for the better part of an hour. Kiyosu's bulletin adds a practical note: the digestive enzymes and the sharp taste live in raw daikon and break down with heat, so grate the top end raw and simmer the thick middle.

The honest part

This is a small trip built on a story. The castle keep is a 1989 reconstruction, not an original building, and if you have seen Nagoya, Inuyama or Hikone, it will not impress you as architecture. The stone is in farmland with no published address, no visitor facilities and nothing to read in English. A radish field in late November looks like a radish field. The farm shop and supermarket that the city names may not have Miyashige on a given day; and the white-necked Daikon Kiyosu is grown largely for school lunches.

Who should skip it: anyone with one day in Nagoya, and anyone who does not cook or care where their food comes from. Who should go: people on a longer stay in Nagoya with a kitchen, people who liked the story of how Edo vegetables were seeded from the provinces, and people who want the Nobunaga castle as an excuse for an afternoon in a part of Aichi that tourists never see.

If you only have one day

A half day, really. Do it on Sunday, December 6, 2026, to catch the castle market.

  1. 8:45Local train from Nagoya Station to JR Kiyosu Station (or Meitetsu to Shin-Kiyosu). Walk 15 minutes to the castle.
  2. 9:30Castle morning market on the promenade (hours are not published; go early). Ask the stalls for 宮重大根. Look at the necks: green is Miyashige, white is Daikon Kiyosu.
  3. 10:15Kiyosu Castle keep (opens 9:00, ¥400). Thirty minutes is enough. Cross the river for the photo of the keep.
  4. 11:00Kiyosu Furusato no Yakata. Ask where the 宮重大根発祥之地趾 is and whether Agri Marché Haruhi is open today.
  5. 11:30If the stone has a pin on your map, take a taxi into Haruhi, look, and have the driver wait. If not, skip it.
  6. 13:00Back to Nagoya for lunch. Carry the radish home in your day bag; a whole one is heavy, so buy a half if they cut them.
  7. 18:00Furofuki at your rental. Grate the top raw.

Running late: drop the stone. The castle market, one radish and the dinner are the trip.
