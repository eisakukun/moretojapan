Shinsaibashi Has No River

You have walked the Shinsaibashi arcade from end to end. Somewhere in the middle you crossed a bridge without noticing. The canal under it was filled in in 1964, and the bridge itself is now in three places, one of them on the far side of the city.

長堀川 Nagahori-gawaShinsaibashi · Nagahoribashi · Yotsubashi · Tsurumi RyokuchiFacts checked November 2026

Shinsaibashi (心斎橋) is the name of a station, a shopping arcade, a department store and half of Minami. The last syllable, -bashi, means bridge. So do the endings of Nagahoribashi, Yotsubashi and Nishi-Ōhashi, three more stations a few hundred metres away. They were all bridges over one canal, the Nagahori-gawa (長堀川), and the subway line that now runs underneath its old bed is named after it.

This piece follows that canal on foot, from the east end to the west, and then takes one train ride to find the oldest piece of it. Nothing here is a sight in the usual sense. It is a way of reading streets you have already walked, plus one iron bridge that most people in Osaka have never heard of.

Why a canal was here at all長堀川 · 1622

Edo-period Osaka was laid out as a grid of hand-dug canals. Merchants paid for many of them, because a canal in front of the warehouse was worth more than a road. The Nagahori canal was dug in 1622 (some accounts say 1625) by four townsmen. One of them was Okada Shinsai (岡田心斎). He built a wooden bridge across it, about 35 metres long, and the bridge took his name: Shinsai's bridge.

The canal ran east to west, from the Higashi-Yokobori canal to the Nishi-Yokobori, and then on towards the bay. What moved on it tells you what the neighbourhood was. On the western stretch, lumber wholesalers dealing in timber from Tosa and Awa (today's Kōchi and Tokushima) lined the banks, and the Tosa domain kept a warehouse compound there. The east bank of the Nishi-Yokobori was a lumber depot too. Upstream, near the east end, the house that later became Sumitomo ran a copper refinery. Its site, in Shimanouchi, was dug up by archaeologists in 1990 when a bank planned to build on it.

The crossing that is legally a bridge心斎橋 · Shinsaibashi

Walk south down the Shinsaibashi-suji arcade from Shinsaibashi Station (心斎橋駅). Where the arcade meets the wide Nagahori-dōri (長堀通) avenue, there is a crossing paved in stone, with a median about 11 metres wide in the middle. The City of Osaka treats this stretch of road as a bridge, and still calls it Shinsaibashi.

Look at the median. The carved stone railings there are reused pieces of the 1909 bridge, the first stone bridge in Osaka, a double arch built when the streetcar line came along the north bank. The gas lamps are reproductions of the originals. The roadway is paved with stone 15 cm thick, so that traffic doesn't crack it.

These railings have moved twice. When the canal was filled in 1964, the stone bridge was taken down and its railings and lamps were rebuilt into a pedestrian overbridge, the one that appears in the 1989 film Black Rain. That overbridge was then removed for the underground mall Crysta Nagahori (クリスタ長堀), which opened in 1997 beneath the avenue, and the stones came down to street level. A plaque recording the first move is now set into the south-east post, on the roadway side.

Getting there: Shinsaibashi Station (心斎橋駅, Midōsuji and Nagahori Tsurumi-ryokuchi lines), then south along the arcade to Nagahori-dōri.
When: Any time; it is a public road. The arcade is quietest before 10:00.
Reality check: It is a busy crossing. Read the plaque from the pedestrian side and don't step into the road for a photo.

One bridge, three bodies木 · 鉄 · 石

"Shinsaibashi" has been three different structures. Only one of them, the last, stayed on this spot, and only in pieces.

Yotsubashi, where four bridges made a square四ツ橋 · Yotsubashi

Walk west along Nagahori-dōri for about ten minutes. Where it meets Yotsubashi-suji, under the elevated Hanshin Expressway, the Nagahori canal once crossed the Nishi-Yokobori canal. Four bridges were built round the junction in a square: Kami-Tsunagi-bashi and Shimo-Tsunagi-bashi over the Nishi-Yokobori, Sumiya-bashi and Yoshinoya-bashi over the Nagahori. You could walk all four sides without touching land. In 1908 the streetcar junction here got the first Japanese-made diamond crossing, where two tracks cut through each other.

The Nishi-Yokobori was filled first, in 1962, and the expressway's northbound loop now runs above its bed. A small site in the middle of the intersection is laid out as the Yotsubashi remains (四ツ橋跡). In the median of Nagahori-dōri stand stones carved with haiku by two eighteenth-century Osaka poets, Konishi Raizan (小西来山) and Uejima Onitsura (上島鬼貫). Onitsura's stone was first put up in 1928 at the east end of Shimo-Tsunagi-bashi, when there was still water to look at.

One caution: sources disagree on where exactly the four bridges stood. One City of Osaka leaflet puts today's crossing a little west of the original; another puts the real spot slightly east, under the expressway. Treat the monument as close, not exact.

Getting there: Yotsubashi Station (四ツ橋駅, Yotsubashi Line), or walk west from Shinsaibashi along Nagahori-dōri.
When: Any time. Daylight helps with the carved text.
Reality check: It is a traffic junction under an expressway. Noisy, grey, and the stones are in the median, so cross at the signals.
The word to look for on markers all along this walk is 橋跡 (hashi ato), "site of a bridge." It is usually on a low stone post at a corner, after the bridge's name.

Nagahoribashi, the east end長堀橋 · Nagahoribashi

Go back east past Shinsaibashi to Sakaisuji. Nagahoribashi (長堀橋) carried that avenue over the canal. A monument stands at the spot, a minute's walk from Nagahoribashi Station, at 1-10 Minami-Senba. It is small and easy to walk past, which is the point of coming: this is the bridge that gave the canal's own name to a station.

From here, the old canal ran a short way further east to join the Higashi-Yokobori, which still has water in it, under the Hanshin Expressway. Stand on any bridge over the Higashi-Yokobori and look west. The gap in the buildings is the line the Nagahori took.

Getting there: Nagahoribashi Station (長堀橋駅, Sakaisuji and Nagahori Tsurumi-ryokuchi lines).
When: Any time.
Reality check: A monument on a city pavement, nothing more. Five minutes.

The iron bridge, out east緑地西橋 · Tsurumi Ryokuchi

The 1873 Shinsaibashi was a wrought-iron bowstring truss, 37.1 metres long, imported from Germany. When the stone bridge replaced it, the iron one was not scrapped. It was reused elsewhere in the city, and in 1989 it was moved to Tsurumi Ryokuchi park (鶴見緑地), the year before the park hosted the 1990 garden expo. It now stands there as Ryokuchi-Nishi-bashi (緑地西橋), the "west bridge of the park." The Japan Society of Civil Engineers lists it as a civil engineering heritage site and calls it the oldest iron bridge still standing in Japan, with its main structure in its original form.

The good part is the train. The Nagahori Tsurumi-ryokuchi Line (長堀鶴見緑地線) runs underneath the old canal through Nishi-Nagahori, Nishi-Ōhashi, Shinsaibashi and Nagahoribashi, then carries on east to Tsurumi-ryokuchi Station. You can board under the stone bridge and get off near the iron one without changing trains.

The park is large. If you are lost, show the kanji and ask staff: 緑地西橋はどこですか (ryokuchi-nishi-bashi wa doko desu ka).
Getting there: Nagahori Tsurumi-ryokuchi Line from Shinsaibashi to Tsurumi-ryokuchi Station (鶴見緑地駅), no change. Fare is a standard Osaka Metro single; check the fare chart at the gate.
When: Daylight. The bridge is in the open park.
Reality check: We could not confirm exactly where in the park the bridge stands. Search 緑地西橋 on a map before you go, and allow time to walk.

The honest part

This is a walk about absence. There is no water, no restored stretch of canal, no museum. Nagahori-dōri is a wide, ordinary avenue with an underground mall below, and most of the "sites" are a stone post, a plaque, or a median strip in traffic. If you need something to look at, stay on the Dōtonbori, which still has its water.

The iron bridge is the real object, and it is a half-day trip for a bridge in a suburban park. Go only if the idea of the same bridge being in two places a century apart pleases you. If it does, it is worth it. If not, the Shinsaibashi crossing alone takes fifteen minutes and costs nothing.

Some details we could not confirm: the plaque's exact wording, the position of the iron bridge inside the park, and the original location of the Yotsubashi square. They are marked above. Check a map before you go.

If you only have one day

A morning on foot along the canal line, an afternoon at the iron end. Flat walking throughout.

  1. 9:30Nagahoribashi Station, exit to Sakaisuji. Find the Nagahoribashi monument at 1-10 Minami-Senba.
  2. 9:50Walk west along Nagahori-dōri. This is the canal bed.
  3. 10:05The Shinsaibashi crossing. Railings, lamps, the plaque on the south-east post. The arcade is still quiet.
  4. 10:30Continue west past Midōsuji to Yotsubashi. The 四ツ橋跡 remains and the haiku stones in the median.
  5. 11:00Walk back east, or go down into Crysta Nagahori and walk under the canal bed instead.
  6. 12:00Lunch around Shinsaibashi.
  7. 13:30Nagahori Tsurumi-ryokuchi Line from Shinsaibashi to Tsurumi-ryokuchi, no change.
  8. 14:15Find Ryokuchi-Nishi-bashi in the park. Walk across the 1873 bridge.
  9. 15:30Same line back to the city.

Running late? Drop Yotsubashi. Shinsaibashi and the iron bridge are the two ends of the story.
