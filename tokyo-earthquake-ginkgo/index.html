The Ginkgo That Outlived 1923

At the top of the stairs from Takebashi Station stands a ginkgo that came through the fires after the Great Kantō Earthquake. For decades it was the tree Japan's weather agency watched to decide when Tokyo's autumn had turned yellow.

震災イチョウ Shinsai IchōTakebashi · Ōtemachi · Kitanomaru ParkFacts checked November 2026

You have probably already done the ginkgo avenue at Jingū Gaien, the four rows of trees that every November photo of Tokyo seems to come from. You may also have walked the Imperial Palace East Gardens. Between those two, at an exit of a subway station most visitors never use, is a single older tree with a better story.

It is called the Shinsai Ichō (震災イチョウ), the Earthquake Ginkgo. It has no queue, no barrier and no light-up. What it has is the reason Tokyo is a ginkgo city at all, and a small role in how the country records the seasons. The trade-off is plain: one tree, not a tunnel of them, and you will spend more time reading than photographing.

The tree at Exit 2震災イチョウ · Shinsai Ichō

On 1 September 1923 the Great Kantō Earthquake struck at lunchtime, and the fires that followed burned out much of central Tokyo. Chiyoda City's tourism site describes this ginkgo as one that escaped those fires when the area around it became burned ground, and calls it a symbol of the capital's reconstruction. Its age is usually given as around 150 to 160 years; nobody has published a planting date.

It did not grow where it stands. It was moved to the moat-side strip now called Ōtemori Ryokuchi (大手濠緑地) when the district was rebuilt. Where exactly it came from is less settled than the tree's fame suggests. Visitors' accounts of the information board place it in Hitotsubashi, near today's Palaceside Building, on the grounds of the Ministry of Education; others say the old Hitotsubashi residence, or near the Mainichi Shimbun building. These are all within a few hundred metres of each other, across the road. Read the board yourself and decide.

Next to it, much taller, is a 4.2-metre bronze of Wake no Kiyomaro (和気清麻呂), an eighth-century courtier. It was unveiled around 1940, the year Japan celebrated the mythical 2,600th anniversary of its founding. The tree is older than the statue and has outlasted the reason the statue was put up.

Getting there: Takebashi Station (竹橋駅), Tōzai Line, Exit 2. The tree is right outside the exit.
When: Any time; it is in a public strip of green. Peak yellow in recent years has come between about 22 November and early December.
Reality check: It is one tree beside a busy road and an expressway ramp. Five minutes is enough unless you are reading every board.

The tree that decided when autumn arrived標本木 · hyōhonboku

Japan's Meteorological Agency records the seasons with real plants. At each weather station it designates a hyōhonboku (標本木), a specimen tree, and an observer looks at it. For ginkgo, the agency's rule is that the "yellowing date" is the first day when most of the leaves on the whole tree have turned yellow. The famous cherry blossom declarations work the same way.

Tokyo's weather station used to be in Ōtemachi, across the road from this moat. Chiyoda City's spot page says the Earthquake Ginkgo was "formerly" the agency's specimen ginkgo for Tokyo. On 2 December 2014 the agency moved the Tokyo observing site from Ōtemachi to Kitanomaru Park, inside the palace grounds a few hundred metres west, and the ginkgo observation now uses a specimen tree there. The tourism page does not say when the role changed hands; the move of the station is the obvious moment, but that is an inference.

So the tree you are standing next to used to be the one that made the news say "Tokyo's ginkgo has turned". The one that does it now is in the park over the wall. The agency does not label it for visitors, and we could not identify which tree it is.

Why the whole city is yellow in December都の木 · to no ki

The usual Japanese explanation for all those ginkgo is fire. The leaves and bark hold a lot of water, the trees were planted on Edo's firebreaks, and after 1923 they were said to have stopped fires spreading in places, so the rebuilt city planted more of them as street trees. That account is repeated everywhere but we did not find a primary source for it, so treat it as the story Tokyo tells about itself rather than proven fire science.

What is documented is the vote. In October 1966 the Tokyo government set up a committee to choose a "tree of Tokyo" and narrowed it to three: zelkova, ginkgo and the Somei-Yoshino cherry. Residents voted by postcard, the ginkgo came first, and it was made the official tree on 8 November 1966. The green Tokyo Metropolitan symbol you see on every bin and bus stop came later, on 1 June 1989. Officially it is three identical arcs hiding the letter T. Almost everyone reads it as a ginkgo leaf.

The yellow has a smell, too. Ginkgo trees are male or female, and the female ones drop ginnan (銀杏), the seeds, whose flesh stinks when crushed underfoot. If a pavement smells like rancid butter in late November, look up. Locals pick the seeds, wash them and roast them; buy them roasted instead, and do not handle the flesh barehanded.

At a yakitori counter in season, ask: ぎんなん、ありますか (ginnan, arimasu ka). Many places skewer and grill them for a few weeks only.

Through Hirakawa Gate to the new specimen平川門から北の丸公園へ · Hirakawa-mon kara Kitanomaru Kōen e

From the tree, Hirakawa Gate (平川門) is a short walk along the moat. It is one of the entrances to the East Gardens, and it was the gate through which, in the Edo period, the dead and the disgraced were taken out of the castle. Go in, walk west through the gardens, and leave by Kita-hanebashi Gate (北桔橋門) towards Kitanomaru Park.

Kitanomaru Park is where the official observing happens now. You will not find a sign saying which tree is the specimen, so do not go looking for one particular trunk. Go for the big ginkgo beside the Nippon Budōkan instead: the Ministry of the Environment's park office singles it out as the park's great ginkgo, its base ringed with stone. In a good week the ground under it is solid yellow.

Getting there: Hirakawa Gate is a few minutes' walk from Takebashi Exit 2 along the moat. Kitanomaru Park is also reached directly from Kudanshita Station (九段下駅), Tōzai, Hanzōmon and Shinjuku lines.
When: The East Gardens are free and need no booking but close on Mondays and Fridays, plus extra days around New Year. Opening hours change by season; check the Imperial Household Agency site before you go. Kitanomaru Park is open all the time.
Reality check: If the East Gardens are closed, walk around the outside of the moat to Kitanomaru instead. It takes longer but you miss nothing about the ginkgo.

The honest part

The Earthquake Ginkgo is not beautiful in the way Jingū Gaien is beautiful. It stands under an elevated expressway next to a traffic junction, and most of the people around it are office workers going to the station. If you come for a picture, the result will look like a tree on a street.

Several things about it are less certain than its fame. Its exact original location, the year it was moved and its precise age all vary between accounts, and the board on site is the best single source you will get. The claim that ginkgo stopped the 1923 fires is a popular explanation, not something we could trace to a study.

Timing matters more than usual. In 2024 the official yellowing did not come until 3 December. Come in mid-November and you may find green; come after the second week of December and the leaves may be on the ground.

If you only have one day

A ginkgo morning on a weekday that is not a Monday or Friday, between about 22 November and 7 December.

  1. 9:00Takebashi Station (竹橋駅), Exit 2. Stand at the Earthquake Ginkgo, read the board, and look across the road at Ōtemachi, where the weather station used to be.
  2. 9:30Walk along the moat to Hirakawa Gate and enter the East Gardens once they open.
  3. 10:45Leave by Kita-hanebashi Gate and cross into Kitanomaru Park. Find the great ginkgo beside the Budōkan.
  4. 12:00Walk out to Kudanshita for lunch. If you see ginnan on a menu, order it.
  5. 13:30Hanzōmon Line from Kudanshita to Aoyama-itchōme, then walk to the Jingū Gaien ginkgo avenue. Now you have seen the survivor and the showpiece on the same day.

Running late? Drop the East Gardens and walk the outside of the moat from the tree to Kitanomaru. Do not drop the tree; it is the reason for the day.
