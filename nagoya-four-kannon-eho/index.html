Nagoya · four temples around the castle · Setsubun, 3 February

Nagoya's Four Guardian Kannon

Everywhere in Japan, Setsubun means facing the year's lucky direction. Only in Nagoya does that direction pick a temple for you: draw a line from the castle, and whichever of four Kannon temples it hits is the one the city crowds into that year. In 2027 it is Jimokuji.

尾張四観音 Owari Shi-KannonKasadera · Arako · Ryūsenji · JimokujiFacts checked November 2026

You have seen Nagoya Castle and its gold dolphins, and you have probably walked the arcades around Ōsu Kannon. The castle and Ōsu are the centre of the city's map. This piece is about four temples on its rim, which the city has treated for centuries as a single set built around that centre.

They are the Owari Shi-Kannon (尾張四観音), the Four Kannon of Owari. Each is a working temple in a residential district with its own reasons to visit. Together they make a small system for choosing where to pray that we have not found explained in English anywhere. The cost: they sit at four corners of a big city, and no single day takes in all of them comfortably.

A compass laid over a castle town恵方参り · ehō mairi

Setsubun (節分), the day before the lunar spring, falls on 3 February in 2027. Across Japan people face that year's ehō (恵方), the direction where the year god Toshitokujin is said to sit, and eat a rolled sushi in silence. The ehō is not random. It follows the first character of the year's sexagenary name, and there are only four possible directions: east-north-east, west-south-west, south-south-east and north-north-west.

Nagoya's version: stand at Nagoya Castle, face the year's ehō, and the Kannon temple nearest that line is that year's ehō no Kannon-sama (恵方の観音様). Visiting it is said to bring the strongest blessing, and that temple's Setsubun is the one that overflows. The other three still hold their own ceremonies.

How the four were chosen is told in more than one way. Aichi Prefecture's tourism site says that after Nagoya Castle was built in 1610 and the town was moved in wholesale from Kiyosu, four temples were set "in the four directions of the calendar" to keep out evil, and calls it an idea found only in Nagoya. Other accounts credit Tokugawa Ieyasu himself. A Nagoya city library note mentions a view that the centre is Ōsu Kannon rather than the castle, and the city's own tourism bureau describes Ōsu as the centre of the four when it promotes Ōsu's Setsubun. The temples themselves are far older than any of this.

Kasadera Kannon: the one that comes round most笠寺観音 · Kasadera Kannon

Kasadera (笠覆寺, Ryūfukuji) in Minami Ward is the ehō temple in four years out of every ten, because south-south-east is assigned to four of the ten year-characters. It was the ehō temple in 2026 and will be again in 2028.

Its founding is put at 733 by the city (another account says 736). The name, "hat temple", comes from a legend: a girl covered a rain-soaked Kannon statue with her own hat, and a nobleman, Fujiwara no Kanehira, saw it and married her. She is remembered as Tamateru-hime (玉照姫). The large wooden main hall is Edo-period and designated by the city. Local histories also place here a hostage exchange involving the boy who would become Tokugawa Ieyasu.

Getting there: Meitetsu Nagoya Main Line to Moto-Kasadera Station (本笠寺駅), 3 minutes' walk.
When: Any day for the grounds. 18 January is its first Kannon day of the year; 9 August is its Kyūman-kusen-nichi (九万九千日) night.
Reality check: On its ehō years Setsubun here is crowded from the night before; in 2026 the eve prayers ran 20:00 to midnight. Off-years are much calmer.

Arako Kannon: twelve hundred carvings by one monk荒子観音 · Arako Kannon

Arako Kannon (荒子観音寺) in Nakagawa Ward is the ehō temple only twice a decade (2030, 2035), and it is the one to go to on any other day. It holds the largest collection of statues by Enkū (円空), the 17th-century wandering monk who carved Buddhas out of scrap wood with a hatchet: 1,255 pieces counted in 2014, many of them only a few centimetres high.

The temple says it was founded in 729 and rebuilt in 1576 by Maeda Toshiie, the warlord from this village who later ruled Kanazawa. Its two-storey Tahōtō pagoda, rebuilt in 1536, is listed as the oldest wooden building in Nagoya and is a national Important Cultural Property.

Getting there: Aonami Line to Arako Station (荒子駅), 10 minutes' walk; or Higashiyama subway line to Takabata Station (高畑駅), Exit 4, 10 minutes.
When: Grounds any day. The Enkū statues are shown, according to Japanese travel guides, only on the second Saturday of each month, with a ¥500 donation. Confirm by phone first (052-361-1778).
Reality check: Come on the wrong Saturday and you see the pagoda and nothing else of Enkū. The phone is answered in Japanese.
On the phone, to check the viewing day: 円空仏の拝観日はいつですか (Enkū-butsu no haikanbi wa itsu desu ka).

Jimokuji: 2027's temple, older than the castle by a thousand years甚目寺観音 · Jimokuji Kannon

Jimokuji (甚目寺), just over the city line in Ama, is the north-north-west temple and the ehō temple for 3 February 2027. Its founding legend puts it in 597: a fisherman named Hadame no Tatsumaro netted a Kannon statue and built a hut for it. Tiles from the late seventh century have been dug up on the site, so the legend is at least pointing at the right era.

What you see is mostly medieval and later. The south gate, Nandaimon (南大門), is early Kamakura and described by the prefecture as the oldest surviving tower gate in Aichi; it, the three-storey pagoda and the east gate are national Important Cultural Properties. Its main image is a hidden statue brought out only on rare occasions; the last two were 2001 and 2012, so do not expect to see it.

Getting there: Meitetsu Tsushima Line from Meitetsu Nagoya to Jimokuji Station (甚目寺駅), 5 minutes' walk.
When: 8:00 to 16:00 daily, per the prefecture. A morning market on the 12th (except August) and a market on the 21st, both 10:00 to 14:00. On 31 December it opens 23:00 to after 2:00.
Reality check: On Setsubun 2026 its bean-throwing prayers ran 8:00 to 17:00. In 2027, as the ehō temple, expect it much busier. The 2027 timetable was not published when we checked.

Ryūsenji: the far one龍泉寺 · Ryūsenji

Ryūsenji (龍泉寺) sits on a hill in Moriyama Ward, east-north-east of the castle, and is the ehō temple in 2029 and 2034. Its tradition traces it to Saichō, the founder of Tendai Buddhism, in the late eighth century, and its main image is a horse-headed Kannon. The Niōmon gate is a national Important Cultural Property, and the city says the temple holds several hundred Buddhist images, beginning with a horse-headed Kannon carved by Enkū. It calls itself the inner sanctuary of Atsuta Shrine.

Getting there: Yutorīto Line guideway bus to Ryūsenji-guchi (竜泉寺口), 3 minutes' walk.
When: Setsubun 2026 ran over two days: eve prayers 20:00 to midnight, then the main festival 9:00 to 18:00.
Reality check: It is the hardest of the four to combine with the others. Give it its own half-day, or leave it.
At any of the four, at the stamp office: 御朱印をお願いします (goshuin o onegai shimasu). Collecting all four is a local habit in itself.

The honest part

Setsubun at the ehō temple is a crowd, not a ritual you watch from close up. Bean-throwing happens from a stage, and the people at the front have paid or queued. If you dislike crowds, go to one of the three non-ehō temples that day, or go to the ehō temple on another day: the direction does not expire on 3 February.

The history is softer than the system looks. Who designated the four, when, and whether the centre is the castle or Ōsu Kannon all vary between sources, and the directions are the almanac's idea of north-north-west, not a line you could rule on a map. Jimokuji in particular sits more west than north if you check a compass.

The four are far apart. Ryūsenji is in the north-east hills, Kasadera in the south, Arako and Jimokuji in the west. Doing all four in one day means a lot of trains and little time at each.

If you only have one day

Three of the four, west to south, on any day (and best on 3 February 2027 if you can stand the crowds at the first stop). Ryūsenji is left for another trip.

  1. 8:30Meitetsu Nagoya Station, Tsushima Line towards Tsushima, to Jimokuji Station (甚目寺駅).
  2. 9:00Jimokuji: the Nandaimon, the pagoda, the main hall. Face the hall: this is 2027's ehō.
  3. 10:30Back to Nagoya; Higashiyama Line to Takabata (高畑駅), walk 10 minutes to Arako Kannon. On the second Saturday, see the Enkū room.
  4. 12:30Lunch near Takabata, then subway back into the centre and Meitetsu to Moto-Kasadera Station (本笠寺駅).
  5. 14:30Kasadera Kannon. Look for Tamateru-hime's story on the boards.
  6. 16:00Finish at Ōsu Kannon, the "centre" in one version of the story, and the arcades around it.

Running late? Drop Arako unless it is the second Saturday. Keep Jimokuji: it is the one whose year is next.
