Edo Bought Its Lottery Tickets at the Temple

From 24 November the queues start forming at Tokyo's lottery booths for the Year-End Jumbo. Two hundred years ago the same crowds stood in temple yards, watching a blindfolded man drive an awl into a box. Where those draws were held, the one ticket that survives, and the stone a shrine put up to remember them.

富くじ tomikujiNihonbashi · Yushima · Yanaka · MeguroFacts checked November 2026

If you are in Tokyo in December, you will see them: lines of people outside small lottery kiosks, banners announcing that this booth sold a winning ticket, older couples comparing numbers. It is the Year-End Jumbo (年末ジャンボ, Nenmatsu Janbo), and in 2026 it goes on sale on 24 November and closes on 25 December.

The habit is much older than the modern lottery. In the early nineteenth century Edo ran public draws at temples and shrines, licensed by the shogunate to pay for repairs, on a schedule busy enough that, by one count, there was a draw somewhere in the city every two or three days. Three temples were so famous for them that they had a collective name. You have probably walked past at least one of them already.

Why this, not Asakusa江戸の三富 · Edo no santomi

Year-end Tokyo is usually sold as Sensō-ji's battledore fair and the bells on New Year's Eve. This is the less photographed side of the same season: money, luck and the shogunate's budget. The places are ordinary working shrines and temples in Nihonbashi, Yushima and Yanaka, all on routes you know. What you give up is spectacle. Two of the most famous lottery sites have no marker at all. You will be reading the history into places that have stopped mentioning it.

How a draw worked富突き · tomi-tsuki

The Japanese name says it: tomi (富), fortune, plus tsuki (突き), a thrust. A temple with a licence printed numbered paper tickets, tomifuda (富札). For each ticket sold, a matching wooden tag went into a large box. On the day of the draw, in the temple grounds and under the eye of the shogunate's commissioners of shrines and temples, the box was shaken and a man with his eyes covered pushed a long awl through a hole in the lid. Whatever tag came out on its point was the winner.

According to Meguro City's history page, tickets cost between one shu and one bu (a sixteenth to a quarter of a gold ryō), and top prizes of 100 ryō later rose as high as 1,000 ryō, with a second prize of 500. The winner did not keep it all. A Japanese travel magazine notes that winners gave ten percent back to the temple, and one account of a 1,000-ryō prize has it shrinking to around 700 after the temple, the ticket seller and expenses had taken their share. There were also prizes for the numbers either side of the winner, the ancestor of today's "front and back prize".

Banned, then licensed for repairs御免富 · gomen-tomi

The shogunate did not like gambling, and it banned lotteries in 1728 because they had got out of hand. It also needed money. Meguro City's history page dates the turn to 1730, when Ninna-ji in Kyoto became the first temple allowed to hold one, because the shogunate could not fund its repairs. Licensed lotteries, gomen-tomi (御免富), "lotteries by permission", became a way to keep temples standing without paying for them.

They peaked in the Bunka and Bunsei eras (1804–1830). Chūō City counts 31 licensed sites in Edo at the height. Three of them became so famous that people called them the Edo no santomi (江戸の三富), the three lotteries of Edo: Kannō-ji in Yanaka, Meguro Fudō and Yushima Tenjin. Then, in March 1842, the Tenpō reforms of the senior councillor Mizuno Tadakuni banned them all. Japan did not hold a public lottery again for a century.

Suginomori Shrine: the stone and the ticket椙森神社 · Suginomori Jinja

Start here, because this is the one place in Tokyo that remembers its lottery on purpose. Suginomori Shrine (椙森神社) is a small Inari shrine among the textile wholesalers of Nihonbashi Horidomechō. Chūō City records that more than fifteen licensed draws were held in its grounds. In 1954 the shrine put up the Tomizuka (富塚), a memorial stone for the lotteries, which stands in the precinct.

The shrine also owns the only Edo lottery ticket designated as a cultural property in Chūō City (since 1994). It is a strip of washi 15.8 by 4.4 centimetres. The city's description of it is a good lesson in how these things were run: it carries a group mark, the number 7238, the date of the draw in 1836, and the line saying the lottery was run by Fudō-in, a temple in Satte, but "held at Suginomori Inari". Temples far from Edo hired a shrine in the busy city to stage their draw.

Do not expect to see the ticket itself. Nothing published says it is on display. The stone is what you come for.

Address: 中央区日本橋堀留町1-10-2. Search "椙森神社". Getting there: A short walk from Ningyōchō Station (人形町駅) or Kodenmachō Station (小伝馬町駅); check the exact route on a map. When: An open neighbourhood shrine. Office hours for charms are not published here; check before you go.

Fukutoku Shrine: the licence, in a shopping complex福徳神社 · Fukutoku Jinja

Ten or fifteen minutes' walk west, Fukutoku Shrine (福徳神社), also called Mebuki Inari, sits among the towers of Nihonbashi Muromachi. Chūō City lists it among the places that held licensed lotteries in Edo. Its modern office has leaned into the story: in 2022 SoraNews24 reported a tomikuji mamori (富くじ守), a lottery charm, at ¥500. Prices and stock change, so treat that as a 2022 figure.

At the charm window, point and ask: 富くじ守をください (tomikuji mamori o kudasai).
Address: 中央区日本橋室町2-4-14. Mitsukoshimae Station (三越前駅) is next to it. Reality check: Busy at lunchtime with office workers. Go mid-morning.

The three that gave Edo its phrase谷中・湯島・目黒 · Yanaka, Yushima, Meguro

Yushima Tenjin (湯島天神) is the shrine you know for exam charms and plum blossoms. According to Meguro City, its lottery started in 1812. Nothing in the grounds, as far as published sources say, marks that it was once one of the three great draws of Edo. Stand in the yard and picture it full of ticket holders instead of students.

Tennō-ji in Yanaka (天王寺) was Kannō-ji (感応寺) until it changed both name and sect. It had the longest relationship with the lottery: a travel magazine says it was allowed to keep holding draws even under the 1728 ban, and from 1748, after a fire, it got permission to hold one every month. It sits at the edge of Yanaka Cemetery, a few minutes from Nippori Station (日暮里駅). Again, no marker.

Meguro Fudō (目黒不動, Ryūsen-ji) is the outlier, far to the south-west. Its draws began in 1812, and Meguro City describes crowds packing the approach on lottery days. If you are already in Meguro, go. It is not worth crossing the city for this alone.

Back to the kiosk年末ジャンボ · Nenmatsu Janbo

The modern lottery is a wartime child. In July 1945 the government sold a lottery called the kachifuda (勝札), "victory ticket", at ten yen; the war ended before it could be drawn. In October 1945 came the first takarakuji (宝くじ), the word you see on every kiosk today. The 1948 law that followed let local governments sell them, which is why the official name of the Jumbo is still a "nationwide local-government lottery".

The 2026 Year-End Jumbo is on sale from 24 November to 25 December, at ¥300 a ticket. The draw is on 31 December at the Tokyo Opera City concert hall. The top prize is ¥700 million, with 23 of them, and ¥150 million for the number either side. Payment of prizes starts on 8 January 2027.

At the kiosk: 年末ジャンボ、バラで10枚ください (nenmatsu janbo, bara de jū-mai kudasai). Bara gets you ten random numbers; renban (連番) gets ten in a row, which is how people try to land the top prize and both either-side prizes together.

The honest part

Two of the three great lottery sites, Yushima and Tennō-ji, have nothing on the ground that mentions the lottery, and the third is a long way out. The Suginomori ticket is described in documents but you will not see it. Fukutoku's charm may or may not be on sale when you go. This is a walk where most of the content is in your head.

The dates in the sources do not agree with each other. Meguro City gives 1817 as the start of Kannō-ji's licensed draws; a Japanese travel magazine has it permitted as early as 1700. This article gives the dates each source states and does not pretend to settle them.

As for the ticket: the odds are what any national lottery's are. Buy one as a souvenir with a story attached, not as a plan. Prizes are paid in Japan, from 8 January, so check the official site for how and where before you count on anything.

If you only have one day

This fits into a morning and early afternoon, ending in Yanaka, where you were going to have a walk anyway.

  1. 9:30Fukutoku Shrine, beside Mitsukoshimae Station. Charm window if it is open.
  2. 10:00Walk east to Suginomori Shrine in Horidomechō. Find the Tomizuka stone.
  3. 10:45Buy a Jumbo ticket at any kiosk on the way (only between 24 November and 25 December).
  4. 11:15Subway to Yushima. Yushima Tenjin, and the courtyard where the draws happened.
  5. 12:15Lunch around Yushima or Ueno-Hirokōji.
  6. 13:30Walk north through Ueno Park into Yanaka (about 35 minutes), or take the train to Nippori. Tennō-ji is at the cemetery's edge.
  7. 14:30Yanaka's streets for the rest of the afternoon.

Running late? Skip Yushima. Suginomori is the one with something to see; Tennō-ji puts you where the afternoon is best.
