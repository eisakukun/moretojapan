Nagoya · Chikusa ward · the 21st of every month

Shikoku in Three Kilometres

Behind Nagoya's Thai-Japanese temple, a full eighty-eight-temple pilgrimage has been folded into a residential hillside since 1909. Most of its halls are shut for twenty-nine days a month. On the 21st, the doors open.

If you have changed trains at Nagoya on the way to Takayama or Ise, you may have spent a spare afternoon at the castle and Ōsu. Ride the Higashiyama Line five more stops past Sakae and you reach Kakuōzan, where Nagoya keeps its oddest temple: one built to hold a share of the Buddha's own remains, sent by the King of Siam, belonging to no single sect.

That temple is the entrance. The subject is what grew up behind it: about eighty small wooden halls scattered through back lanes, a cemetery and people's side yards, each standing in for one of the eighty-eight temples of the Shikoku pilgrimage. You give up a famous route and a guided path. You get the 1,200-kilometre Shikoku circuit compressed into an afternoon, kept going by a few elderly volunteers, and opened properly only on the 21st.

Why a Thai relic temple sits on a Nagoya hill覚王山日泰寺 · Kakuōzan Nittai-ji

In 1898 a British official digging in northern India found an urn of bone fragments inscribed as relics of the Buddha. They went to the King of Siam in 1899, and in 1900 a portion came to Japan together with a gilt-bronze Buddha statue. Where to house them took two more years to settle. Nagoya was chosen in 1902, and the relics waited at Banshō-ji in the city centre while a site was found.

The site chosen in 1903 was a slope called Tsukimizaka in Tashiro village, then outside the city. A local history published by Sumitomo Mitsui Trust says the deciding reason was money and land put up by the village head, Katō Keiji, with the crossing of two old pilgrim and trade roads as a bonus. The temple opened in 1904 as Kakuōzan Nissen-ji (日暹寺): Nichi for Japan, Sen for Siam. Siam became Thailand in 1939, and in 1942 the temple renamed itself Nittai-ji (日泰寺) to match. Kakuōzan, "Mountain of the Enlightened King", is the temple's mountain-name, and the station and the whole district took it.

Because the relics were a gift to Japanese Buddhism as a whole, the temple belongs to none of its sects. Abbots come in turn from nineteen different schools, three years each. The reliquary tower behind the main hall, designed by the architect Itō Chūta, was finished in 1918; the main hall you see now dates from 1984.

A Shikoku you can walk before lunch写し霊場 · utsushi reijō

The Shikoku pilgrimage takes you round 88 temples linked to Kūkai (Kōbō Daishi) on a loop of about 1,200 kilometres. For most people in 1909 that meant weeks away from work. So, as across Japan in that era, local backers built a copy (utsushi) on their doorstep. According to the Chūnichi Shimbun, prominent Nagoya figures placed small halls on land east of the temple from 1909, each holding sand brought from its matching temple in Shikoku. The idea is that praying over that sand stands in for standing at the original temple. A local blogger who walked the route dates the building from August 1909 into the early Taishō years.

The halls are tiny: one to four tsubo, roughly the floor of a parking space or two. A 1999 survey by the Japanese site Sukima Man'yūki found some solidly built ones near the start, a run of identical boxes lined up "like a housing estate" in a cemetery, and others half-collapsed. No published source we found names the founders individually, and no one we could find gives a current count of halls still standing. Check before you go if completeness matters to you.

Why the numbers jump around札所 · fudasho

The hill the founders used was thick woodland. It is now houses, a widened road and a municipal cemetery, and the halls had to move with it. The Chūnichi reports that after the war some halls were lived in by people bombed out of their homes, and that road widening forced others out. One walker estimated that about forty percent of the halls have been relocated, some only a few metres, some a long way, after the road from Himegaike to Jiyūgaoka was widened and the temple buildings were rebuilt.

The result is that the order no longer runs neatly round the hill. Hall 1 is south of the temple, beside a rest hut. The route was laid out from the start to go round in the opposite direction to what you might expect. The 1999 survey divided the halls into areas lettered A to H, with the last cluster north of the temple across Himegaike-dōri, some of the 50s still in their original order, and one block inside the cemetery. Walkers agree on one point: without a map you will get lost. Both people who wrote up the walk recommend downloading or printing one first.

Why it has to be the 21st弘法さん · Kōbō-san

The 21st is Kūkai's day each month, and at Nittai-ji it is the Kōbō-san fair. Aichi Prefecture's tourism site counts about a hundred stalls along the approach and in the grounds: household goods, food, plants, clothing. The fair is the reason the halls open. Most have no permanent attendant. On the 21st, volunteers sit in them.

In 1999 the Sukima writer found women sitting in hall after hall, singing pilgrim hymns (go-eika) and serving visitors, and houses along the route opening their entrances to offer refreshments. They also found most halls already shut by 14:00. In 2018 the Chūnichi profiled Iwata Toshio, then 77, who with his wife had kept the rest hut at Hall 1 since April 2008, pouring tea for anyone who came by. At that point only a dozen or so halls still had a keeper, and a long-time guide put the number who walk the full circuit at about fifty. A 2014 walker warns that even on the 21st not every hall is open, and that weekend and holiday mornings are the best bet.

Monday 21 December 2026 is the last 21st of the year. It is a weekday, so expect fewer open doors than on a weekend fair, and go early.

At the hut by Hall 1, ask which way to start: どちらから回りますか？ (dochira kara mawarimasu ka?)
If someone is sitting in a hall, ask before stepping up to it: お参りしてもいいですか？ (omairi shite mo ii desu ka?)
Getting there: Nagoya Station (名古屋駅) → Higashiyama Line (東山線), direct to Kakuōzan Station (覚王山駅), about 15 minutes. Fare by IC card; check the exact amount in the app. Then 10–15 minutes' walk up the approach.
When: the 21st of each month. Temple gates 5:00–16:30, every day, free. Temple stamp (goshuin) desk 9:00–14:00.
Reality check: the temple does not run the 88 halls and staff may not be able to help. Hall opening depends on volunteers. Plan to finish by early afternoon.

What to look at on the way見どころ · midokoro

Inside the halls you will usually find a small image of the principal Buddha of the matching Shikoku temple and a number plaque. The 1999 survey singled out a hall of a thousand Jizō near the start, a "make-up Kannon" (化粧観音, Keshō Kannon) at number 65, a pond shrine to Benten beside Sairen-ji, and a few stray halls near the reliquary tower that survived from an older layout. Treat the halls as small shrines in someone's care rather than sights: several stand on what is plainly private ground.

The reliquary tower itself (奉安塔, Hōantō) is worth the short climb behind the main hall: a stone stupa in a Gandhāran style, set in a quiet wood, very unlike anything else in Nagoya.

The honest part

This is a shabby, unfinished, half-forgotten thing, and that is the point of it. The Chūnichi's own reporter described a place gone quiet and tired, with stone Buddhas standing alone. Some halls are rotting. Some numbers are missing. You will spend real time hunting for a box behind a hedge and may not find it. If you want the better-known version of a miniature pilgrimage, Kyoto's Omuro route behind Ninna-ji is the one in English guides.

On a weekday 21st, many halls may stay shut. Keepers are elderly and the Chūnichi's 2018 profile already talked about who would take over. Information here comes from articles dated 1999 to 2018 plus the prefecture's current listing; what is open in December 2026 is not something anyone publishes. The fair's stalls are a neighbourhood market, not a crafts market: vegetables, cheap clothes, snacks. It is a 2½- to 3-hour walk on pavement and slopes, ending on the far side of the temple from where you began.

If you only have one day

  1. 8:15Higashiyama Line from Nagoya Station to Kakuōzan Station.
  2. 8:40Walk up the approach while the stalls set up. Bow at the main hall, then find Hall 1 and its rest hut just south of the temple.
  3. 9:00Start the circuit with your map. Take the halls in number order where you can; skip a missing number rather than search for it.
  4. 11:45Finish at the last halls north of Himegaike-dōri. Walk back to the temple and up to the reliquary tower.
  5. 12:30Lunch from the stalls or the cafés along the approach. Stamp desk closes at 14:00.
  6. 14:00Back to Kakuōzan Station; you are in Nagoya Station by 14:30 for an afternoon train.

Running late? Do Halls 1 to 4 near the start, which the 1999 survey found close together and in order, then go straight to the reliquary tower.
