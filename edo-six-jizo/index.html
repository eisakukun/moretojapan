Six Bronze Gatekeepers at the Edges of Edo

Between 1708 and 1720 a Fukagawa monk had six seated Jizō, each about the height of a ceiling, cast in bronze and set at the six roads out of the city. Five are still sitting there. You have probably walked past at least one.

If you have been to Sugamo for the old-people's shopping street, you walked past a bronze Jizō taller than a ceiling at the entrance and kept going to the famous one further in. If you went to Kiyosumi Garden, there was another a short walk away. If you stayed near Shinjuku Gyoen, a third. None of them has an English sign that tells you it has five siblings.

They are the Edo Roku Jizō (江戸六地蔵), the Six Jizō of Edo: a set of seated bronze statues of the bodhisattva who looks after travellers, children and the dead, each placed where a main road left the city. Taken together they draw the edge of eighteenth-century Edo on a modern map of Tokyo.

Why this, not the Togenuki Jizōa city boundary you can walk

Sugamo's Togenuki Jizō at Kōganji is the one with the queue and the English pages. The six bronzes are less visited and less explained, and the set has no single place to visit: they are spread from Shinagawa to Asakusa. In exchange you get a reason to go to five neighbourhoods in one day, and a way to see where Edo ended on each side.

Who made them, and how地蔵坊正元 · Jizōbō Shōgen

The project started in 1706 with a monk known as Jizōbō Shōgen (地蔵坊正元) of Fukagawa. Japanese sources tell the reason two ways. One version is a vow made after prayers for recovery from illness were answered. The other, given by the Sugamo shopping-street association, stresses protecting travellers at the six entrances to the city. Japanese sources also say he was following Kyoto's older set of six Jizō on the roads out of that city.

He paid for it by public subscription. Kōtō City's cultural-property page for the Fukagawa statue records that it was cast in April 1717 with Shōgen as sponsor, by the caster Ōta Suruga-no-kami Fujiwara no Masanori (太田駿河守藤原正儀) of Kanda Nabe-chō, and that about 10,000 donors' names are engraved on it. That is the thing to look for when you visit: the bronze is covered in names.

The same page explains the construction. The figure was not poured in one go. Thin, separately cast copper parts were joined by casting metal into the seams, and the pedestal was built the same way. A six-statue programme over fourteen years made sense for one workshop only because the statues were built from parts.

No. 1 · Shinagawa品川寺 · Honsenji · old Tōkaidō

The first statue, cast in 1708, sits by the gate of Honsenji (品川寺, read Honsenji, not "Shinagawa-dera") on the old Tōkaidō, the road to Kyoto. The temple's own site calls it nearly three metres tall and lists it as a Tokyo-designated treasure. Shinagawa was the first post town out of Edo, so this one saw off everyone heading west.

Getting there: Aomono-yokochō Station (青物横丁駅), Keikyū Line, about 5 min walk. 南品川3-5-17.
When: The statue is outdoors by the gate.
Reality check: Temple hours are not published online; go in daylight.

No. 2 · Higashi-Asakusa東禅寺 · Tōzenji · Ōshū Kaidō

Cast in 1710 for the road north toward Nikkō and Ōshū. Tōzenji is in the flat blocks north of Sensō-ji that most visitors never reach, which is exactly why it is worth the walk: this was the northern edge.

Getting there: 台東区東浅草2-12-13. Japanese sources give about 14 min on foot from Minowa Station (三ノ輪駅) on the Hibiya Line; check on a map.
When: Statue at the front of the temple.
Reality check: The furthest of the five from a station. No English signage.

No. 3 · Shinjuku太宗寺 · Taisōji · Kōshū Kaidō

Cast in 1712 and sitting in Taisōji on the Kōshū road west, in what was the post town of Naitō-Shinjuku. The temple is also the Naitō family temple and keeps a large Enma, the judge of the dead (more on that in our Shinjuku piece).

Getting there: 新宿2-9-2. Shinjuku-gyoemmae Station (新宿御苑前駅), Marunouchi Line, about 2 min.
When: Statue outdoors in the grounds.
Reality check: The Enma hall opens only in mid-January and mid-July.

No. 4 · Sugamo真性寺 · Shinshōji · Nakasendō

Finished in September 1714 and placed on the Nakasendō, the inland road to Kyoto. The Sugamo shopping-street association gives it as 2.68 m tall, 3.45 m with its lotus pedestal, holding a staff and wearing a large hat. Every 24 June the temple holds a memorial for the six with a rosary 16 metres long, 541 beads of cherry wood, passed hand to hand by several hundred people.

Sugamo has two Jizō that everybody means. At the station end of the street, ask: 江戸六地蔵はどちらですか (edo roku jizō wa dochira desu ka). Otherwise you will be sent to the Togenuki Jizō.
Getting there: 豊島区巣鴨3-21-21, at the Sugamo Station (巣鴨駅) end of Jizō-dōri.
When: Outdoors. Memorial with the giant rosary: 24 June.
Reality check: The street is crowded on the 4th, 14th and 24th of each month, the Togenuki Jizō's fair days.

No. 5 · Fukagawa霊巌寺 · Reiganji · Mito Kaidō

Cast in April 1717. This is the statue Kōtō City describes in detail: the 10,000 engraved names, the joined parts, a Tokyo designation in 1970. Reiganji is a short walk from Kiyosumi Garden and the Fukagawa Edo Museum, so it is the easiest to add to a day you may already have planned.

Getting there: 江東区白河1-3-32. Kiyosumi-shirakawa Station (清澄白河駅), Hanzōmon and Ōedo lines, about 3 min.
When: Outdoors in the grounds.
Reality check: Easy to miss if you go straight to the garden.

No. 6 · the one that is gone永代寺 · Eitaiji · Chiba Kaidō

The last statue, cast in 1720, stood at Eitaiji in Fukagawa, the large temple attached to Tomioka Hachiman Shrine, for the road east to Chiba. In 1868 the new government separated Buddhist temples from Shinto shrines, Eitaiji was abolished and demolished, and the statue went with it. The small temple called Eitaiji today was refounded later under the old name. Japanese sources say believers had a replacement sixth statue made, now at Jōmyōin (浄名院) in Ueno; this could not be confirmed with the temple.

The honest part

Each statue is a five-minute stop. Seeing all five is a day of trains for about twenty-five minutes of standing in front of bronze, and they look alike. If you are not interested in where Edo ended, do one: Reiganji with Kiyosumi Garden, or Shinshōji with Sugamo.

There is almost no English on site, and the numbering on signs, if any, may not match the casting order used here. Japanese pilgrimage lists number them in a different, walking order.

Some of the story rests on thin sources. The donor count and construction method are documented for the Fukagawa statue by Kōtō City; the reasons for the vow, the Kyoto model and the replacement sixth statue come from secondary Japanese sources. The Tokyo cultural-property database, which holds the official descriptions, was offline for renovation when this was checked.

If you only have one day

Clockwise from the south, on a weekday that is not the 4th, 14th or 24th. Train times are not given; leave the gaps as they are.

  1. 9:00Aomono-yokochō Station (青物横丁駅). No. 1 at Honsenji, then a short walk along the old Tōkaidō.
  2. 10:30Kiyosumi-shirakawa Station (清澄白河駅). No. 5 at Reiganji. Look for the names on the bronze.
  3. 11:15Kiyosumi Garden or the Fukagawa Edo Museum, then lunch near the station.
  4. 13:30Minowa Station (三ノ輪駅), then the walk to No. 2 at Tōzenji in Higashi-Asakusa.
  5. 15:00Sugamo Station (巣鴨駅). No. 4 at Shinshōji, at the near end of Jizō-dōri. Walk the street afterwards.
  6. 16:30Shinjuku-gyoemmae Station (新宿御苑前駅). No. 3 at Taisōji, the last one before dark in December.

Running late? Drop Tōzenji. It is the furthest from a station, and the other four still give you the south, east, north and west edges.
