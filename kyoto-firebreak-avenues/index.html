Kyoto Tore Down Its Own Streets

Everyone repeats that Kyoto was spared in the war. Then you cross Oike-dori, fifty metres of asphalt in a city of alleys, and nobody explains it. The width is a scar: more than ten thousand houses the city pulled down itself, in 1944 and 1945, so that fire from bombs that mostly never came would have nowhere to jump.

You have taken the bus down Horikawa-dori to Nijō Castle (二条城, Nijō-jō). You have walked from Kyoto City Hall to the river along Oike-dori, and crossed Gojō-dori to get to Kiyomizu. All three felt like a different city from the lanes of Gion and Nishiki: wide, straight, built for cars, the kind of road you stop noticing.

Those three roads are the most visible thing the Second World War left in Kyoto, and they were not made by bombs. They were made by an order to clear houses, carried out by the city on its own neighbourhoods. This piece is about reading them, plus the two places in Kyoto where bombs really did fall, which very few visitors go to.

Why this, not the temples京都は焼けなかった？ · Kyōto wa yakenakatta?

The usual story about Kyoto and the war is about what was saved: the temples, the wooden townhouses, the reason you came. That story is mostly true and it hides two things. Kyoto was bombed, on a small scale, more than once. And Kyoto prepared for being burned by destroying parts of itself first. The temples are the half of the story you have already seen. This is the other half, and it is lying in plain sight under your bus.

The trade-off is simple. Nothing here is beautiful in the guidebook sense. You get roads, a small park, and a stone. What you get in return is a way of seeing the city that will stay with you every time you cross one of these avenues again.

What "building evacuation" meant建物疎開 · tatemono sokai

Sokai (疎開) is usually translated "evacuation", and in wartime Japan it was applied to children, factories and buildings. A building evacuation meant demolishing rows of houses to open firebreaks, strips of empty ground that a fire caused by incendiary bombs could not cross. Japanese cities were dense and wooden. The planners' logic was that you could not stop the bombs, but you could cut the city into blocks small enough that each fire would burn out on its own.

In Kyoto, according to a Kyoto University study of what happened to the cleared land, building evacuations were carried out from July 1944, more than ten thousand houses were destroyed, and the cleared sites added up to about 115 hectares. The same study notes that a fourth round of demolitions began in late July 1945 and was still under way on 15 August, the day the war ended. Some households lost their homes in the last weeks of a war that did not then come to Kyoto.

Oike-dori: one side of a street, 70 metres deep御池通 · Oike-dōri

Before the war, Oike was not an avenue. Kyoto City's own history page traces it to a narrow Heian-era lane, Sanjō Bōmon kōji (三条坊門小路). In March 1945, the houses on the south side of the street, from the west bank of the Kamo River to Horikawa-dori, were evacuated and pulled down, in a strip about 70 metres deep. The purpose, in the city's words, was to stop fire spreading from air raids.

After the war the cleared ground was not handed back. Japan's Home Ministry instructed in August 1945 that evacuated sites should be kept as open land for city planning, and Kyoto did exactly that. In 1947 the strip became a city-planned road 50 metres wide, finished in 1953. It is the widest street in Kyoto, which is why, since the late 1950s and 1960s (Shōwa 30s), the Gion Matsuri float parade has turned onto it. The Tōzai subway line was dug under it in 1997, and the pavements were rebuilt as the "Oike Symbol Road" by 2003.

The thing to look at is the asymmetry. The clearance took the south side. When you walk Oike from City Hall towards Horikawa, look at the depth of the blocks on each side and where the older lanes stop. You are walking on what were back gardens and kitchens in the spring of 1945.

Getting there: Kyoto Shiyakusho-mae Station (京都市役所前駅), Tōzai Line, is under the east end. Walk west on the south pavement to Horikawa-dori, about 1.7 km. When: Any time. Weekday mornings have the least traffic noise. Reality check: There is no plaque about the evacuation on Oike. You are reading the street, not a sign.

Gojō and Horikawa五条通・堀川通 · Gojō-dōri, Horikawa-dōri

The same thing happened, by most accounts, on the two other avenues you know. On Gojō-dori, houses between Higashiōji-dori and Senbon-dori were removed to make a fire-blocking belt, and after the war the road was widened to about 50 metres on the cleared land. Horikawa-dori was cleared the same way; the city's own guide to its bridges counts Horikawa, Gojō and Oike as the three trunk roads built after the war on these sites, to ease traffic in the centre.

The street-by-street dates for Gojō and Horikawa are harder to pin down in published sources than Oike's, so this article does not give them. What is clear is the pattern. Kyoto's postwar recovery did not have to rebuild burned districts the way Tokyo, Osaka or Nagoya did. One Japanese historian of the period argues that in Kyoto, the "reconstruction" was largely the job of deciding what to do with land the city had cleared itself.

Gojō is the one to stand on. Walk onto Gojō-Ōhashi (五条大橋), the bridge over the Kamo, and look west: the avenue runs straight and wide, and when you turn round, the old lanes of Higashiyama climb up towards Kiyomizu in their original width. Horikawa is easiest to see from the bus to Nijō Castle, which drives down the middle of it.

Where the bombs did fall: Nishijin西陣空襲 · Nishijin kūshū

Kyoto was not untouched. A Ritsumeikan University study counts seven places in the city damaged by bombing between January and June 1945. The heaviest was in Nishijin, the weaving district, on 26 June 1945. The stone that records it stands in Tatsumi Park (辰巳公園, Tatsumi Kōen), a small neighbourhood park that was itself made on the bombed ground.

The inscription cites the Nishijin police record: 43 killed outright, 13 seriously injured, 53 lightly injured, 109 casualties in all. The stone was put up in August 2005 by a local committee. There is nothing else here: a playground, benches, ordinary houses on every side. It is a ten-minute walk from Horikawa-dori, so it fits between Nijō Castle and the Nishijin textile centre.

The kanji to look for on the stone are 空爆 (kūbaku, aerial bombing) and 被災 (hisai, suffered a disaster). The full title reads 空爆被災を記録する碑, "stone recording the bombing".

Address: 上京区智恵光院通下長者町上ル西辰巳町 (Kamigyō-ku, Nishi-Tatsumichō). Search "辰巳公園 上京区" in a map app. Getting there: City bus along Horikawa-dori to the Nishijin area, then about 10 minutes west on foot. Check the bus stop on a route planner. When: An open public park. Visit in daylight and keep your voice down; people live around it.

Umamachi: the first one馬町空襲 · Umamachi kūshū

The first bombs on Kyoto fell on the night of 16 January 1945 on Umamachi (馬町), in Higashiyama, east of the river between Gojō and Shichijō. The Kyoto Prefectural archives put the dead at over thirty; a Kyoto City document gives 34 to more than 40, with over 50 injured. Kyoto remembers it together with Nishijin as the two raids.

This article cannot point you to a memorial at Umamachi: published sources reviewed for it did not give a location for one. If you are walking from Gojō-Ōhashi to the Kyoto National Museum or Sanjūsangen-dō, you are passing through the district. Knowing the date is enough to change how that walk feels.

The honest part

This is a walk along traffic. Oike and Gojō are loud, exposed, and in summer there is very little shade. The Nishijin stone is small and you could walk past it. There is no museum of the building evacuations, no audio guide, and no English signage anywhere on this route.

Some of the details are thin. Kyoto City documents Oike's clearance clearly. For Gojō and Horikawa, the exact dates and depths of each clearance are not in any source checked for this article, and nothing here should be read as more precise than that. If you want the numbers, the Japanese research by Kawaguchi Tomoko on Kyoto's building evacuations is the place to start, and it is in Japanese.

Finally, people died in the places on this map, and others lost their homes to their own city. Treat the park and the bridge as what they are, not as photo spots for a "dark tourism" story.

If you only have one day

Half a day is enough. This runs north to south and finishes near the Gojō side of Higashiyama, where you can carry on to Kiyomizu or the museums.

  1. 9:00Start at Tatsumi Park in Nishijin. Read the stone. Walk east to Horikawa-dori.
  2. 9:40Bus or walk south down Horikawa to Nijō Castle. You are travelling on a cleared avenue the whole way.
  3. 10:20At the corner of Horikawa and Oike, turn east. Walk Oike on the south pavement to City Hall, watching the block depths.
  4. 11:15Coffee near City Hall, then south on Kawaramachi or the subway to Gojō.
  5. 12:00Walk onto Gojō-Ōhashi. Look west down the wide road, then east up the narrow slopes of Higashiyama.
  6. 12:30Lunch. Then walk south-east through Umamachi towards Sanjūsangen-dō or the National Museum, knowing what happened here on 16 January 1945.

Running late? Drop Nishijin and start at Nijō Castle. Oike and Gojō are the two you should not skip.
