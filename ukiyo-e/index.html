Tokyo · a trade, not a style

The Blue Came From Berlin

Ukiyo-e was a mass-market product made by four people and sold for the price of a bowl of noodles.

Seven places in Tokyo where Edo's print trade is still operating — plus why no museum can promise you the Great Wave, what the margins of a print actually tell you, and why the Fuji above every bathhouse is not descended from Hokusai at all.

Reading + walkingOne dayMuseums, workshops, one shopRyōgoku · Harajuku · Jimbōchō

You have seen the wave. On a tote bag, on a phone case, on the wall of a ramen shop in your own country. You may even have queued for a Hokusai exhibition and shuffled past the real sheet in a crowd three deep.

This is about the other half of that object: the trade that produced it, which is still operating in Tokyo, in buildings you can walk into on a Tuesday. Six of the seven places below sit on or just outside the Yamanote loop. None of them is a blockbuster.

One thing to settle first, because it changes how you look at everything else. A ukiyo-e print is not a painting. It is a commercial product made by at least four people who never met the customer, sold for roughly what a bowl of noodles cost, and printed in the hundreds. Treating it as a lost masterpiece is a nineteenth-century European habit, not an Edo one.

The blue is an import, and that is the whole storyすみだ北斎美術館 · Sumida Hokusai Museum

Start in Ryōgoku, ten minutes on foot from the sumo stadium. Hokusai was born in this part of the city and barely left it. He is said to have moved house 93 times — a figure repeated everywhere, including by the Ōta Memorial Museum, which also points out that nobody has ever verified it — and to have changed his working name about thirty times, which is much better attested. Almost every one of those addresses was inside the old Katsushika district, the low ground now covered by Sumida, Katsushika, Kōtō and Edogawa wards. The museum is small, modern, aluminium-clad, and stands on the block where he lived.

Go for one thing: the blue. That deep, flat, slightly cold blue running through Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji is Prussian blue — a synthetic pigment first made in Berlin in the early 1700s. It reached Japan through the Dutch trading post at Dejima, and for decades it was a luxury, used in thin washes. In the late 1820s the price fell far enough that a publisher could lay it across a whole sheet, thousands of times over, and still sell cheap. Hokusai's Fuji series appears almost immediately afterwards.

The workshops called it bero-ai (ベロ藍) — "Berlin indigo", worn down to a two-syllable shop word. The most Japanese image in the world is built on a German chemical that became affordable about two years before it was used.

The permanent gallery is small and leans on touchscreens. The temporary exhibitions are where the real sheets are.

Getting there: Ryōgoku Station (両国駅), JR Sōbu line — 7 min on foot. Toei Ōedo line Ryōgoku Station — 5 min.
When: 9:30–17:30, last entry 17:00. Closed Mondays (the next weekday if Monday is a holiday) and New Year.
Admission: ¥400 for the permanent collection. Temporary exhibitions are priced separately.
Allow an hour, not three. When a special exhibition is on, the permanent floor is the smaller half of the visit.

There is no "the" Great Wave, and no museum can promise you one太田記念美術館 · Ōta Memorial Museum of Art

Three minutes behind the Harajuku crowds, in a basement and two quiet rooms, sits one of the best ukiyo-e collections in private hands. Its own website says, flatly: this museum has no permanent display.

That is not modesty. Paper and vegetable dye fade under light, permanently and cumulatively, so prints are shown for a few weeks and then rested for years. Exhibitions here usually run in two halves with a complete swap of every work between them, and the museum shuts for several days in between to rehang. Those closures are not short: an eight-day one ran from late September into early October 2026.

The word to look for on the calendar is 展示替え (tenji-gae), rehang. It means the doors are locked.

While you are there, notice how many of the best things are not prints at all. The autumn 2026 exhibition is built around a night scene by Katsushika Ōi (葛飾応為), Hokusai's daughter, who ran his studio, almost certainly painted parts of work signed by him, and has only about ten surviving works under her own name. Hers is a 肉筆 (nikuhitsu) piece — brush-painted, one of one. A label with that word on it means you are looking at something nobody could ever reprint.

At the desk, asking whether a particular work is in this half of the show 前期ですか、後期ですか zenki desu ka, kōki desu ka
Getting there: Harajuku Station (原宿駅), JR Yamanote, Omotesandō exit — 5 min. Meiji-jingūmae Station (明治神宮前駅), Chiyoda / Fukutoshin lines, exit 5 — 3 min.
When: 10:30–17:30, last entry 17:00. Closed Mondays, plus the rehang days. Check the calendar on the site before you fix a date.

The margin tells you more than the picture

Most visitors look at the image and ignore the four centimetres around it. That is where the metadata is.

From 1790, prints in Edo had to carry a censor's seal before they could be sold, and the system changed shape several times over the following seventy years. For a long middle stretch, sheets carry both an approval seal and a date seal giving an era year and a month. A print is therefore not "nineteenth century". It is "the fifth month of 1858", and you can read that off the paper with no expertise at all.

Also in the margin: the publisher's mark, which is a small logo rather than a name. The publisher took the financial risk, so the publisher got the branding. The artist's signature sits inside the picture, where it reads as part of the design.

You cannot test any of this behind museum glass, where the sheet is matted and the margins are hidden. The place to actually look is a dealer's shop, with the whole sheet in your hands.

The workshops never closed

This is the part most people get wrong. Woodblock printing in Tokyo is not a revived craft. The trade contracted hard once photography and lithography arrived, but it never stopped, and three working houses will let you in.

Adachi Institute of Woodcut Prints (アダチ版画研究所) in Shimo-ochiai has been at it since 1928 and employs its own carvers and printers. The showroom displays the production stages and sells new impressions, cut and printed by hand from newly carved blocks. These are honest reproductions, not fakes, and they are priced like furniture rather than like antiques.

Takahashi Kōbō (高橋工房) in Bunkyō has been printing since the Ansei era, in the 1850s. They sell, and they run hands-on sessions — but the official listing carries the line 来訪前に要連絡: contact before you visit. Treat that as binding, not as a formality.

Mokuhankan (木版館) in Asakusa is the shop founded by the Canadian printmaker David Bull, five minutes off the Nakamise approach. You can handle prints and talk to whoever is behind the counter. Note that the hands-on "print parties" that made the shop famous online are no longer advertised on its own site — do not build a day around one without phoning first.

An original costs less than a concert ticket大屋書房 · Ohya Shobō

Walk into the corner shop at Surugadai-shita in Jimbōchō, the second-hand book district. Founded 1882, fourth generation, specialising in Edo-period books, old maps and ukiyo-e. They stock Hokusai, Hiroshige, Kuniyoshi, Yoshitoshi, Kunichika, Eisen.

Prices start in the low thousands of yen for late-Edo and Meiji sheets and climb to whatever a good Hokusai costs, which is a different conversation entirely. The point is that the floor is low. People assume originals are museum-only objects. They were mass-market objects, and a great many of them survived.

Their English site says it plainly: Many items are not listed online. We warmly invite you to visit our shop in person. Take that literally — there is far more in the boxes than on the walls.

Pointing at a category rather than a specific sheet 実物を見せていただけますか jitsubutsu o misete itadakemasu ka
Getting there: Jimbōchō Station (神保町駅), Hanzōmon / Mita / Shinjuku lines — 3 min. 1-1 Kanda-Jimbōchō, Chiyoda-ku.
When: Monday to Saturday, 10:00–18:00. Closed Sundays.
A working antiquarian dealer, not a souvenir shop. Browsing is welcome, handling is on request, and most of the affordable ukiyo-e are late Edo and Meiji rather than the famous 1830s names.

Three different things get sold as "ukiyo-e". The gap between them is not a secret — it is just rarely spelled out on a label.

The Fuji on the bathhouse wall is not descended from Hokusai銭湯 · sentō

Every visitor who steps into an old Tokyo bathhouse sees the same thing painted above the tubs: Mount Fuji, blue sky, water in the foreground. It looks like the obvious descendant of the print tradition. It is not.

The convention starts in 1912, at a bathhouse called Kikai-yu in Kanda Sarugaku-chō. The owner hired a painter, Kawagoe Kōshirō, to decorate the wall above the bath. Kawagoe came from Shizuoka, so he painted his home mountain. Customers liked it, other bathhouses copied it, and a century later it is effectively a rule. One man's hometown became a national default.

What makes it worth a detour now is a very specific kind of scarcity. Japan currently has three working bathhouse mural painters: Maruyama Kiyoto, Nakajima Shigeo, and Tanaka Mizuki, the only one under sixty. The murals are painted straight onto the wall, over the previous one, every few years, while the bathhouse stays open. Nothing is conserved. The entire surviving output of this art form at any moment is whatever happens to be on the walls this year.

Hokusai did not invent manga, and the man who did has a free museumさいたま市立漫画会館

Hokusai Manga (北斎漫画), first volume 1814, is a sketchbook: fifteen volumes of figures, animals, waves, ghosts, people pulling faces. It is wonderful, and it is not comics. The drawings do not connect to one another, and nothing unfolds over time. In 1814 the word manga meant something closer to "sketches, drawn as they come".

The modern meaning arrives with Kitazawa Rakuten (北澤楽天, 1876–1955), generally described as Japan's first professional cartoonist, who used the word in its current sense and worked in the lineage of imported Western political cartooning as much as anything Japanese.

The site of his house in Saitama is now the Saitama Municipal Cartoon Art Museum, opened in 1966 — the first public museum in Japan devoted to manga. It is small, it is free, and it stands five minutes from the station in Bonsai-chō (盆栽町), the district founded by Tokyo bonsai nurserymen who moved out of the city after the 1923 earthquake. You can do the museum and several bonsai gardens in one afternoon, which is a strange and very good combination.

Getting there: Ōmiya-kōen Station (大宮公園駅), Tobu Urban Park line — 5 min on foot. Or Toro Station (土呂駅), JR Utsunomiya line, east exit — 15 min. Ōmiya is about 30 minutes from Tokyo, then one or two stops.
When: 9:00–16:30. Closed Mondays, the day after a public holiday, and New Year.
Admission: free.
It is one converted house. Forty minutes is enough. Go for Rakuten and stay for the bonsai, not the other way round.

The honest part

The Great Wave is almost certainly not on display anywhere in Tokyo on the day you visit, and no amount of planning fixes that. Even the Sumida Hokusai Museum rotates its holdings. If seeing that one object is the goal, you are better served by the British Museum or the Met — which is an uncomfortable thing to write about Tokyo.

Real prints are disappointing at first. A standard ōban sheet is about 39 × 26 cm, and it sits behind glass in a deliberately dim room. Every reproduction you have seen was photographed under bright light and boosted. The originals are quieter and often browner, because the paper has aged and the reds have shifted.

These museums are small. The Ōta is two rooms. Sumida Hokusai is an hour. If you arrive expecting a national gallery you will be back out on the street before your eyes have adjusted to the light.

The two working workshops are businesses with deadlines, not attractions. Adachi closes Sunday and Monday. Takahashi requires contact in advance, in writing, probably in Japanese. Neither is set up to absorb drop-in visitors who want to watch someone work.

And the Ōta Memorial is cash only, in 2026, in Harajuku. Bring notes.

If you only have one day

A weekday. Not a Monday — the Ōta and Sumida Hokusai are both shut. Not a Sunday either, if you want the shops.

Running late? Drop Jimbōchō — the shop keeps the longest hours of the three and is the easiest to pick up another day. Do not drop the Ōta, because its closed days are the hardest to work around.

If you would rather see the trade than the collection, swap Jimbōchō for Adachi in Shimo-ochiai: Ryōgoku → Akihabara → Yamanote to Mejiro, then ten minutes on foot. That only works Tuesday to Saturday.
