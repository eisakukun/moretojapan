The Street That Sells Sweets by the Box

Walk north from Endōji's arcade, cross the six-lane Route 200, and you are in what was once called the biggest cheap-sweets wholesale district in Japan. Its name, Meidōchō, is no longer on any address. It survives on a crossing, a bus stop and an expressway ramp, and on the cardboard boxes stacked outside the shops.

明道町 Meidōchō · 新道 ShindōNishi Ward, NagoyaFacts checked November 2026

You have probably done Nagoya the efficient way: the castle, a miso-katsu lunch, maybe the Endōji shopping arcade and the old warehouse street of Shikemichi because someone told you they were the "real" Nagoya. They are. But most visitors turn back at the big road on Endōji's northern edge. Across it is a grid of plain streets where shopfronts are walls of cardboard: ramune candy, fake cigarettes, puffed corn sticks, fruit-gum strips, sold by the case to the people who run Japan's sweet shops, school events and festival stalls.

This is a working wholesale district, not a theme street. The trade-off is clear: no English, very early hours, closed on Sundays, many shops that do not want photos. What you get is the supply side of the dagashi (駄菓子) you have seen in nostalgic shops in Tokyo, and the reason so much of it is made around one corner of Nagoya.

A name that is only on signs明道町 · Meidōchō

Meidōchō was a town name in Nishi Ward. The addresses here are now Shindō (新道) and Habashita (幅下), and Japanese guides are blunt that Meidōchō survives only as a nickname and on the Meidōchō crossing, the city bus stop of the same name, and the Meidō junction and ramp on the Nagoya Expressway. Two of the best-known shops stand almost under the junction.

The name Shindō, "new road," has a simpler story. A 2017 local walking class run by Dai-Nagoya University traced it to a side street opened off the old Minoji (美濃路), the highway to Gifu, for merchants' use. Where the two roads meet there is still a cluster of temples.

Getting there: Sengenchō Station (浅間町駅, Tsurumai subway line), 6 to 10 minutes on foot depending on the source. Or city bus from Nagoya Station (名古屋駅) bus stop 9 (Kan-Meieki 1, toward Kami-Iida or Ōzone), about 10 minutes, to Meidōchō stop.
Also: Kokusai Center Station (国際センター駅, Sakura-dōri line) is about 15 minutes; Nagoya Station's Sakura-dōri exit about 20.

Why sweets, why here菓子問屋街 · kashi ton'ya-gai

The origin stories are stories, and the Japanese sources say so. One has sweets being sold to the workers who built Nagoya Castle. The version repeated most often, including by the city's volunteer guides, is that in the Edo period low-ranking samurai made rice crackers and boiled sweets as piecework and sold them to travellers on the Minoji. Nobody I found gives a date.

The step that made it national is better attested across sources: after the Great Kantō Earthquake of 1923, Nagoya makers supplied sweets to the children of devastated Tokyo, and the district grew into a wholesale centre for the whole country. One Nagoya food site gives a figure of more than 130 manufacturers by the early Shōwa period. After the war, two shared market buildings opened: the Chūkyō Kashi Gangu Oroshi Ichiba (中京菓子玩具卸市場, sweets and toys) in 1948 and the Chūō Kashi Oroshi Ichiba (中央菓子卸市場) in 1951.

The makers are still around. Japanese business press lists sweet and snack companies headquartered in Nishi Ward, among them Marukawa (the gumball maker), Kakudai (Kuppī ramune), meito and Kasugai. Aichi Prefecture, on 2021 government figures, was second only to Tokyo in confectionery sales, and first in shipments of hard candy and of biscuits and dry sweets.

What is left: the shops問屋 · ton'ya

The number of wholesalers has fallen sharply, and the shared market buildings have closed. A 2026 Japanese housing-site feature says the joint buildings were taken down and that a local group has put up an explanation board on the site of the 1948 market. Other write-ups still describe a shop working at the entrance of the 1951 market. I could not settle which is true now, so do not plan around the markets.

Most stock comes in packs. To ask whether you can buy just one: ばらで 買えますか (bara de kaemasu ka).
If you want the whole box: これ、ひと箱 ください (kore, hito-hako kudasai).
When: Monday to Saturday mornings. Several shops open by 8:00 and close by 17:30; everything listed is closed on Sundays.
Reality check: These are businesses serving trade buyers. Cash or card is not stated anywhere; bring cash. Hours are from 2025–26 write-ups and may have changed.

Who actually buys here嫁菓子 · yome-gashi

Watching the customers explains the district. Japanese features mention parents buying for neighbourhood children's associations (子ども会, kodomo-kai) and town festivals, families in the summer holidays, people stocking a party. A Nagoya food site adds an older use: Meidōchō was a standard place to buy yome-gashi, "bride sweets," the bags of sweets thrown or handed out from a bride's house in the Nagoya custom of kashi-maki (菓子撒き). Fewer families do it at home now, the site says, though it turns up at weddings.

Sumida Shrine and the candy factory counter隅田神社 · Sumida Jinja / 歌舞伎飴本舗

Next to the site of the 1951 market is Sumida Shrine (隅田神社), founded at the start of the Genroku era, in 1688. Local guides call it the guardian of the sweets district; one says it was founded after a fever epidemic and a fire. It is a small neighbourhood shrine and the easiest landmark for getting your bearings.

The volunteer guides also point to Kabuki-ame Honpo (歌舞伎飴本舗), a candy maker where you can buy at the factory reception. They give its hours as 8:30–18:00, closed Sundays, holidays and the second Saturday. No source gives its address, so ask at a shop or check a map app before you go.

Reality check: The volunteer guide site warns that many shops and the markets do not allow photos, and that this is a place of business, not a tourist site. Ask before you raise a phone: 写真を撮ってもいいですか (shashin o totte mo ii desu ka). A no is normal.

The honest part

It is smaller than its reputation. "Japan's biggest" is a past-tense claim; Japanese write-ups describe a district that has shrunk a lot, with a few shops and a lot of ordinary houses and offices between them. If you come expecting a lane of nostalgic sweet shops like the ones in old Tokyo, you will be disappointed. These are warehouses with the door open.

Timing is unforgiving. Sunday is dead. Afternoons get thin as shops close from 17:00. Nobody here has time to explain products in English, and a lot of what is on the shelf is sold as twenty or thirty pieces, which is a lot of corn sticks to carry home in a suitcase.

And the facts have gaps. The Edo origin is a story. The status of the old market buildings is contradictory between sources. Shop hours come from 2025–26 articles, not from shop websites.

If you only have one day

Do it as a morning, then fold it into the Endōji and Shikemichi walk you might already be planning. Not on a Sunday.

  1. 9:00Arrive at Sengenchō Station. Walk south toward the Meidōchō crossing.
  2. 9:15Tatsuya and Sakurai Shōten under the Meidō junction. Buy here; this is the main event.
  3. 10:15Sugiyama and Aramaki in the Shindō streets. Ask before buying or photographing.
  4. 11:00Sumida Shrine and the site of the 1951 market in Habashita.
  5. 11:45Cross Route 200 south into Endōji arcade for lunch. Our piece on Endōji covers that street.
  6. 13:30Shikemichi warehouses along the Horikawa.
  7. 15:00Walk to Nagoya Station, about 15 to 20 minutes, with a bag of sweets nobody at home will recognise.

Running late? Keep Tatsuya and Sakurai, skip the rest of Shindō, and go straight to Endōji.
