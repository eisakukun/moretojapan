Ueno · Tokyo · one morning in 1868

Ueno Was a Battlefield

The museums are there because the ground was empty. The ground was empty because the government burned it.

A four-hour walk through the 1868 Battle of Ueno — two gates full of bullet holes, a grave with the name left off, and a statue standing twenty metres from the men it defeated. Free, outdoors, and almost entirely unsigned in English.

Walking guide≈4 hoursFree, outdoorsUeno · Minami-Senju

You have been to Ueno Park. The National Museum, the pandas, the cherry trees, the queue outside a special exhibition. It reads as a cultural campus — museums, a concert hall, a zoo, a pond — the sort of place a city plans on purpose.

It was not planned. It is a burn scar. On one morning in July 1868 the new imperial government shelled the temple complex that owned this entire hill, burned most of it down before dark, and confiscated the land. The museums are here because the ground was empty, and the ground was empty because of what happened on it.

This walk is free, outdoors, and takes about four hours. What you are looking at is stone, two wooden gates, and a hill. If you need a spectacle, this is not that. If you want to understand why Tokyo's most institutional park has an unnamed mass grave in the middle of it, this is the walk.

Start on something much older摺鉢山古墳 · Suribachiyama Kofun

Two minutes from the Saigō statue there is a low mound with benches on top and office workers eating konbini lunch. It is a keyhole tomb from the late 5th century — 70 metres long, about 5 metres high, roughly 1,500 years old. Haniwa fragments and Sue ware have come out of it.

It was not alone. A cluster of burial mounds ran from here across the ground now occupied by the Tokyo National Museum. This is the only one left; the others went under the things built on top of them. Nobody stops here. There is a small sign, a set of steps, and no fence.

Start here because it sets the pattern for the day: in Ueno, each era built directly on the previous one and removed most of it.

Getting there: Ueno Station (上野駅), Park Exit — 6 min on foot, left of the main path
When: always open · free

The hill belonged to a temple built as a copy of Kyoto寛永寺 · Kan'eiji

In 1625 the monk Tenkai founded Kan'eiji here, on the northeast side of Edo Castle — the unlucky direction, the one that needed a guardian. He named the mountain Tōeizan: the Mt Hiei of the East. The reference is exact and deliberate. Kyoto had Enryakuji on Mt Hiei; Edo would have its own.

This was politics, not piety: a new capital, still raw, borrowing the sacred landscape of the old one. At its height Kan'eiji held essentially all of what is now the park and a good deal beyond it, and six Tokugawa shoguns are buried in its precincts.

Kiyomizu Kannon-dō, built in 1631, is one of the few buildings that survived 1868 — the same wood you would have seen before the shelling. The looping pine in front of it, the Moon Pine, is a reconstruction: the original went in a typhoon in the early Meiji years and existed only inside a Hiroshige print until the temple rebuilt it in 2012.

Getting there: in the park above the pond, 5 min from Suribachiyama
When: grounds open daily · free

What happened on 4 July 1868

By the summer of 1868 the shogun had already surrendered Edo Castle without a fight. A holdout force of former retainers, the Shōgitai, refused to accept it and dug in on this hill, around the temple, under the protection of the abbot — an imperial prince.

The government commander, Ōmura Masujirō, attacked at about eight in the morning with roughly 2,000 troops from three directions. Satsuma troops under Saigō Takamori took the front, at the Black Gate. But the decisive part was not the infantry.

Saga domain's Armstrong guns were set up on the high ground at the Kaga estate in Hongō — today the University of Tokyo campus — and fired across Shinobazu Pond into the hill from around midday. It was finished the same afternoon: 266 Shōgitai dead recorded, and around 30 on the government side.

Stand at the western edge of Shinobazu Pond and look up and away from the park, towards Hongō. That low ridge across the water is where the guns were. The pond is the range. It is the only part of this story you can see without a sign explaining it.

A note on the date: Japanese sources give this as the 15th day of the 5th month of Keiō 4 — the old lunar calendar. In the Gregorian calendar that is 4 July 1868. Same morning, two calendars.

Two gates were shot at. Both are still standing.

The temple headquarters' gate stands in the park, in front of the Rinnōden hall next to the Tokyo National Museum. Kan'eiji's main administrative compound stood exactly where the museum stands now; it burned, and this gate is what is left of it. There are bullet holes in the doors — small, dark, unlabelled in English. Look at the door panels at chest height rather than at the structure.

The Black Gate, where the heaviest fighting happened, is not in Ueno at all.

In 1907 the Black Gate was moved to Ensōji in Minami-Senju, three stops away, and it is still riddled. The temple has it because after the battle the government refused to let anyone collect the Shōgitai bodies; they were left on the hill as a warning. The priests of Ensōji cremated them at Ueno and buried the remains here.

This is a working neighbourhood temple, not a site. No admission, no English, no exhibit, and nobody expecting you.

A grave with the name left off彰義隊戦死者碑

Behind the Saigō statue, on the terrace at the top of the hill, there is a stone marker for the Shōgitai dead, put up in 1882 by survivors — Ogawa Okisato led the effort, and his family kept the plot for generations afterwards.

Read the stone. The calligraphy is by Yamaoka Tesshū, himself a former shogunal retainer, and it says only 戦死之墓 — "grave of the war dead." The words Shōgitai appear nowhere on it. Fourteen years after the battle, naming the losing side in public was still not possible. The omission is the point, and it is the most articulate thing in the park.

Getting there: top of the hill, behind and to the right of the Saigō statue
When: always visible · free

The statue of the man who commanded the attack, twenty metres away

The bronze Saigō Takamori in a light kimono, walking his dog, was unveiled on 18 December 1898. Takamura Kōun made the figure; Gotō Sadayuki made the dog.

Two things are worth knowing while you stand there. First: Saigō led the force that attacked this hill, and the grave of the men he defeated is directly behind him. Second: by the time the statue was cast, he had himself died as a rebel against the same government, in 1877, and was pardoned only posthumously.

That is why he is in casual clothes with a dog and not in uniform — a way to commemorate the man without commemorating either war. His widow, Itoko, is said to have objected at the unveiling that her husband was not like this. The most photographed statue in Ueno is a careful political compromise, standing on the exact spot where the compromise was needed.

Why the empty ground became museums

After the fire, the land was confiscated. In 1872 the army planned to put a military hospital and a military cemetery on it. A Dutch military doctor teaching medicine in Japan, Bauduin, argued against it — the hill should stay green and public — and the plan was dropped. In 1873 a government decree designated Ueno as one of Japan's first public parks, along with Shiba, Asakusa, Fukagawa and Asukayama. The museum and the zoo followed within a decade. There is a small monument to Bauduin in the park, on the pond side.

One last fragment, five minutes from the statue: the Ueno Great Buddha (上野大仏) is now a face on a wall and nothing else. The body went in earthquakes and then to a wartime metal drive; only the face was kept. Because the face "cannot fall any further," students come here to pray before exams. Different disasters, same Ueno logic — what survives is a piece of something, kept and repurposed.

The honest part

This walk is stones, two gates and a view across a pond. The bullet holes are the size of a fingertip and there is no interpretation panel in English at either gate. Nothing has been reconstructed for visitors and nothing is dramatised. If you go expecting a battlefield site in the European or American sense — a visitor centre, a map with arrows — you will be disappointed inside ten minutes.

The Minami-Senju detour is a 25-minute round trip for a twelve-minute visit, at a temple that owes you nothing and is not set up for guests. Go quietly, or skip it and see the park gate instead; they were part of the same wall.

Cherry blossom season is the worst possible time for this. The terrace by the statue and the grave becomes a drinking party — which is its own comment on the place, but you will not be able to stand and read anything.
