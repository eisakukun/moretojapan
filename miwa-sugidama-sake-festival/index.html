Where Every Cedar Ball Comes From

The green ball hanging over a sake brewery's door started at one shrine in Nara, under a mountain that is itself the god. On 13 November the shrine hangs a new 200-kilogram one. On the 14th, brewers from across Japan come to pray in front of it.

大神神社 · 酒まつり · 杉玉Sakurai, NaraFacts checked November 2026

You have seen the ball. It hangs from the eaves in Fushimi, in Takayama, in Saijō, over the door of every sake shop that wants you to know it is one: a sphere of cedar sprigs, sometimes green, sometimes brown. You may even know what it is called, sugidama (杉玉). What the English guides tend to leave out is where it comes from, and that you can stand under the original.

That place is Ōmiwa Shrine (大神神社, Ōmiwa Jinja) in Sakurai, south of Nara by local train. If you have walked Nara Park, Ōmiwa is the next step: older, quieter, and the one shrine in Japan that the brewing trade treats as head office. This week is the week it renews its sign.

The trade-off is plain. There is no famous building to photograph. The shrine has no main hall at all, because the god lives in the mountain behind it. The thing you are going to look at is a ball of cedar, and the festival is a rite performed for people who make sake for a living. If that sounds thin, read the next section first.

A sign that tells you how old the sake is杉玉 · 酒林

The ball is also called sakabayashi (酒林), "sake forest". Breweries hang a fresh, bright green one when the new sake of the season is ready. Through the year the cedar dries, and by autumn it has turned brown, which is the season for hiyaoroshi, the batch that rested over summer. The colour of the ball over the door is a rough calendar of what is in the tanks.

Why cedar? Because the cedars of Mt Miwa (三輪山, Miwayama) are the god's own trees. The Man'yōshū poems already speak of "the sacred cedars of Miwa". Sake sellers later hung cedar from Ōmiwa's god as a mark of their trade, and the ball shape followed. Ōmiwa still makes sugidama and sends them out to breweries and sake shops across the country, each with a wooden tag that reads 三輪明神・しるしの杉玉: "the sign-ball of the Miwa deity".

The 13th: a new ball, 1.5 metres across大杉玉の掛け替え

Two very large sugidama hang on the shrine's main halls, the one you will stand in front of being the worship hall (拝殿, haiden). The shrine gives their size as about 1.5 metres in diameter and about 200 kilograms. On 13 November, the day before the festival, they come down and fresh green ones go up. On the 14th, the brewers who come to pray see a ball that has been on the building for less than a day.

The shrine does not publish a time for the changeover, so plan on seeing the result rather than the lifting. That also means this is not a one-day trip: the new ball will be green for weeks. If you are in Nara at any point between mid-November and the new year, you will see it at its freshest.

When: new ball hung on 13 Nov; time not published. It stays up for the year.
Where: the worship hall (拝殿) of Ōmiwa Shrine, 奈良県桜井市三輪1422.
Reality check: no ticket, no fee to enter the grounds. The ball is the whole sight; give it ten minutes, not an hour.

The 14th: the brewers' festival醸造安全祈願祭（酒まつり）

The official name is Jōzō Anzen Kigansai (醸造安全祈願祭), a prayer for a safe brewing season. Everyone calls it the sake festival, sake matsuri (酒まつり). In 2026 it falls on Saturday 14 November and starts at 10:30 in front of the worship hall. An hour earlier, at 9:30, there is a smaller rite for Emperor Sujin at a side shrine; the next section explains why he is there.

The people in the seats are tōji (杜氏), master brewers, and others from the trade. Serai, a Japanese magazine, puts the number at around 200. After the prayers, four shrine maidens dance Umazake Miwa no Mai (うま酒みわの舞), holding sprigs of cedar instead of bells. Nara Prefecture's own description adds that the brewers then receive staffs dressed with sugidama to take back to their breweries. It is a working rite. You are watching an industry ask for a good winter.

Since 1 November the shrine has also been holding a national sake display, 全国銘酒展, in the south-west corridor of the courtyard, planned to run until the 14th. Barrels and bottles sent by breweries are stacked as offerings, so this is the one morning to read the labels of half of Japan in one place. Serai also mentions sake being poured for visitors in the grounds; the shrine does not say how much or when.

When: Sat 14 Nov 2026, 10:30, in front of the worship hall (拝殿). Sujin rite 9:30 at 天皇社.
Cost: free.
Reality check: this is a ceremony for the trade, not a show. Seats are for participants; expect to stand at the back of the courtyard. No published rule on photography: watch what others do, and never during the prayers.

The first brewer has his own shrine活日神社

The story is in the Nihon Shoki, the chronicle compiled in 720. In the reign of Emperor Sujin (崇神天皇), a plague was killing the country. The god of Mt Miwa, Ōmononushi (大物主大神), told the emperor in a dream to make offerings, sake among them. A man named Takahashi Ikuhi (高橋活日命) brewed it, the plague passed, and Ikuhi said, in the chronicle's account, that the sake was not his: the god of Miwa had brewed it. That is why the emperor gets a rite on festival morning, and why Ōmiwa's god is the god of sake.

Ikuhi is worshipped as the ancestor of every tōji. His small shrine, Ikuhi Shrine (活日神社, Ikuhi Jinja), stands inside the Ōmiwa grounds, north of the worship hall along the start of the Yamanobe Trail (山の辺の道, Yamanobe no Michi) and up a flight of stone steps. It was once called Hitoyozake no Yashiro (一夜酒の社), the shrine of the one-night sake, after the claim that he brewed it overnight.

If you lose the path, ask anyone in shrine dress: 活日神社はどちらですか (Ikuhi-jinja wa dochira desu ka). They will point you north.
Getting there: from the worship hall, walk north on the Yamanobe Trail and take the stone steps up.
Reality check: it is a small shrine. On the 14th it may be the quietest place in the grounds, which is the point.

The mountain with no hall三輪山 · 三諸

Walk to the worship hall and look for the main hall behind it. There isn't one. Mt Miwa is the object of worship, so the hall faces the mountain directly.

The language explains the rest. Nara Prefecture's notes on the shrine say the mountain was also called Mimuro-yama, written 三諸, characters read as "the origin of sake", and that both the character for god, 神, and offerings of sake were once read miwa. Miwa the place, miwa the god and miwa the sake were one word. The sugidama is a pun you can hang on a door.

The mountain itself can be climbed from the auxiliary shrine Sai Jinja (狭井神社). The English guides cover the climb well, so this piece does not; on festival morning, do the shrine first and the climb another day.

A brewery on the approach今西酒造 · 三輪伝承蔵

Imanishi Shuzō (今西酒造) brews in Miwa itself. Its local label is 三諸杉 (Mimurosugi), and the one it sells to specialist shops across Japan is みむろ杉, the same name in a softer script: the shrine's mountain and the god's cedar in one word. On the shrine approach it runs a shop and a standing bar, the Miwa Denshō-gura (三輪伝承蔵). A Japanese magazine lists a "Miwa set" of 90 ml of sake with a small dish and a glass of water from ¥1,100. Hours and prices are from secondary sources: check before you go.

At the counter: 三輪セットをお願いします (Miwa setto o onegai shimasu).
Getting there: on the approach between Miwa Station and the shrine.
When: hours not confirmed for 2026. On festival day expect it to be busy.
Reality check: a 90 ml pour is a taste, not a session. You are drinking at 11:30 in the morning next to a shrine; pace yourself for the walk back.
Getting there: JR Nara Station (奈良駅) → local train on the JR Man'yō-Mahoroba Line (万葉まほろば線, the Sakurai Line) → Miwa Station (三輪駅), then about 5 min on foot. Trains on this line are local services; look up times on a route app before you leave Nara. From Kyoto, go to Nara first.
Reality check: the shrine's bus to Sakurai Station stopped running at the end of March 2026. Do not plan around it.

The honest part

The festival is a ritual you will watch from behind rows of participants. You cannot hear most of it, you cannot read the prayers, and nobody is performing for you. If you want a festival with floats, this is the wrong one.

The sake poured for visitors may be small, may run out, and is not announced. Do not make the trip for free sake.

The changeover of the big ball on the 13th has no published time, and I could not find a reliable account of when it happens. If you go on the 13th hoping to see it lifted, you are gambling.

I also could not confirm whether visitors can receive one of the small sugidama the shrine makes. They are sent to breweries and shops. If you want to ask, do it at the amulet counter, and take no for an answer:

小さい杉玉はありますか (chiisai sugidama wa arimasu ka).

Finally, Saturday 14 November is the middle of autumn-leaf weekends. Trains out of Nara will be busier than usual. That is a reason to leave early, not to skip it.

If you only have one day

Saturday 14 November 2026, starting from Nara.

  1. 8:30Leave JR Nara Station on the Man'yō-Mahoroba Line toward Sakurai. Choose a train that reaches Miwa by about 9:15.
  2. 9:15Walk the approach. Look up at the shop eaves for cedar balls.
  3. 9:30Go to the worship hall and stand under the new ball while the Sujin rite is on at the side shrine. Find your spot for 10:30 now.
  4. 10:30Sake festival. Stay for the cedar dance.
  5. 11:20The national sake display in the south-west corridor, on its last day.
  6. 11:45North along the Yamanobe Trail to Ikuhi Shrine. Five quiet minutes with the first brewer.
  7. 12:30Back down the approach to Imanishi's Miwa Denshō-gura for one pour, then lunch.
  8. 14:00Train back to Nara. If you are running late, drop the sake display, not Ikuhi Shrine.

Can't make the 14th? Do the same walk any day until the new year, without the rite. The ball will still be green.
