Pumpkins for the Shortest Day

Around the winter solstice, two Kyoto temples hand out pumpkin. One is a nunnery deep in Ōhara. The other is a narrow temple on the Teramachi arcade you have probably walked past, whose Jizō is said to go into hell on your behalf.

冬至のかぼちゃ tōji no kabochaTeramachi · ŌharaFacts checked November 2026

If you have shopped the covered Teramachi arcade (寺町通) north of Shijō, you have walked within a few metres of Yatadera (矢田寺). It is a single hall set back from the shopfronts, with lanterns over the entrance, and most visitors take it for a souvenir stop. On 23 December a very large pumpkin sits in front of that hall, and people queue to stroke it.

This is not a festival in the sense of crowds and stalls. It is a small, practical winter custom, and in Kyoto two temples keep it publicly. The pair works well as a reason to spend a December day away from the autumn-leaf hangover at the famous gardens.

Why pumpkin, and why that week冬至 · tōji

The winter solstice in 2026 falls on Tuesday 22 December, at 5:50 in the morning Japan time. The old belief, repeated in both temples' own announcements, is that eating pumpkin on the solstice protects you from chūbu (中風), the paralysis that follows a stroke, and from illness in general.

Ōhara's nunnery serves its pumpkin in the four days leading up to the solstice. Yatadera holds its memorial the day after. That gap matters if you have only one day: you cannot do both events on the same date.

Yatadera and the Jizō who takes your place矢田寺 · Yatadera

Yatadera is a branch of the older Yatadera in Yamato, in today's Nara Prefecture. The City of Kyoto's account, based on the temple's own tradition, says it was founded in 845 near Gojō, moved in the fourteenth century to the area still called Yata-chō, and came to its present address in 1590 when Toyotomi Hideyoshi pushed Kyoto's temples into a line along Teramachi. That is why a temple sits in the middle of a shopping street: the street was named after the temples, not the other way round.

The main image is a Jizō called Daijuku Jizō (代受苦地蔵), the Jizō who takes on suffering in your place. The story is that the monk Mankei (満慶) was taken to the underworld, saw a monk standing in the flames of hell pulling sinners out, and carved the figure from what he had seen. This is the figure the pumpkin is offered to.

On 23 December the giant pumpkin is placed in front of the main hall, and visitors stroke it as a prayer. Take-home pumpkins are given out for a fee; the price was not published when we checked. A 2015 report describes visitors being served pumpkin simmered sweet and salty. The 2026 announcement does not mention this, so don't count on it.

When you reach the table for the take-home pumpkin: かぼちゃをひとつください (kabocha o hitotsu kudasai). Have coins ready.
Getting there: Kyoto-Shiyakusho-mae Station (京都市役所前駅, Tōzai Line), 6 minutes' walk south down Teramachi.
When: Wednesday 23 December 2026, 10:00–15:00.
Reality check: The temple is small and the arcade narrow. Take-home pumpkin is paid, price unpublished. Temple phone 075-241-3608 (Japanese).

The picture-scroll talk絵解き · etoki

Alongside the memorial, Yatadera runs an etoki (絵解き), a sermon given by pointing at a painted scroll and narrating it scene by scene. The scroll is the Yata Jizō Engi Emaki (矢田地蔵縁起絵巻), the illustrated story of the Jizō, which is a nationally designated Important Cultural Property. The ticket includes a take-home pumpkin.

Places are limited and taken by advance booking. The application form was due to open at the end of October, and it closes when full. The talk is in Japanese, and the pictures do much of the work, but this is the one part of the day where the language barrier is real.

How to book: The online form is linked from the Kyoto City tourism event page for かぼちゃ供養【矢田寺】. Price not published there.
Reality check: Japanese only. By late November it may already be full; check before you plan around it.

The bell that sends the dead back送り鐘 · okurigane

Yatadera is also one half of a pair you may already know the other half of. Across the river, Rokudō Chinnōji (六道珍皇寺) rings a "welcoming bell" at the start of Obon in August to call the dead home. Yatadera's bell is the okurigane (送り鐘), the "sending bell," rung on 16 August to send them back. Its founder story even overlaps: Ono no Takamura, the courtier said to have commuted to the underworld through a well at Chinnōji, is named as Yatadera's co-founder.

None of this happens in December. But it explains why this tiny temple keeps a winter memorial at all. Its whole identity is the boundary between the living and the dead, and the pumpkin is offered at that boundary.

Jakkōin's pumpkin, in Ōhara寂光院 · Jakkōin

Jakkōin (寂光院) is the nunnery at the end of a valley in Ōhara where Kenreimon-in, the mother of the drowned child emperor Antoku, lived out her life after the Taira clan's defeat in 1185. If you have been to Ōhara, you probably went to Sanzen-in on the other side of the valley. Jakkōin is the quieter one.

For the four days to the solstice, the temple runs Hokkori okabocha-daki (ほっこりおかぼちゃ焚き), roughly "warming simmered pumpkin." Note the polite o- on okabocha: it is the Kyoto habit of putting honorifics on food. Simmered pumpkin is served to visitors free of charge from 9:30 each morning until it runs out. You still pay the normal admission to enter.

The sign to look for at the gate, written on a board or banner: おかぼちゃ焚き (okabocha-daki). If you see 終了 (shūryō) next to it, the pot is empty for the day.
Getting there: Kyoto Bus to Ōhara (大原) stop, then 15 minutes' walk. It is a long bus ride from the city; check the Kyoto Bus timetable for the day.
When: 19–22 December 2026, temple open 9:00–16:30. Pumpkin from 9:30 until gone.
Admission: adults ¥600, high school ¥600, junior high ¥350, elementary ¥100. Pumpkin free. Phone 075-744-3341.
Reality check: "Until gone" means arriving late may mean no pumpkin. Ōhara is noticeably colder than central Kyoto.

The honest part

This is a bowl of pumpkin and a large vegetable to touch. If you need spectacle, December in Kyoto has better options. What you get is a custom that is still done for its own sake, in two places that most visitors only see in autumn.

The two events don't overlap, and Ōhara is a long way out by bus, so trying to do both in a day doesn't work. Yatadera's picture-scroll talk is in Japanese and may be fully booked. The take-home pumpkin price and whether simmered pumpkin is served at Yatadera this year were not published when we checked. Both temples are small: on the weekend days at Jakkōin, expect a queue.

If you only have one day

Make it Tuesday 22 December, the solstice itself: pumpkin in Ōhara in the morning, Teramachi in the afternoon to see Yatadera's Jizō the day before the memorial.

  1. 7:45Kyoto Bus from the city towards Ōhara. Check the timetable the night before and pick a bus that reaches Ōhara before 9:15; the pumpkin runs out.
  2. 9:15Ōhara bus stop, walk 15 minutes up the valley to Jakkōin.
  3. 9:30Pumpkin service starts. Eat, then see the main hall and garden.
  4. 11:00Walk back down the valley; lunch in Ōhara.
  5. 12:30Bus back to the city.
  6. 14:30Kyoto-Shiyakusho-mae Station, walk south down Teramachi to Yatadera. Pay respects to the Daijuku Jizō.
  7. 15:30Keep walking the arcade south to Shijō. If you can stay one more night, be back at Yatadera at 10:00 on the 23rd for the pumpkin itself.

Running late? Drop the Ōhara lunch and eat in the city. Don't drop the early bus.
